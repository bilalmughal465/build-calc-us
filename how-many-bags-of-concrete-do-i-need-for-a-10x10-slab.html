How Many Bags of Concrete Do I Need for a 10x10 Slab? A Worked Project Walkthrough

One 10 ft x 10 ft patio slab, worked from the tape measure to the order: the volume, the bag count for every bag size, the water, the gravel base, and the honest bags-vs-truck cost comparison — so you order once and do not run short.

Short answer

A 10 ft × 10 ft slab 4 in thick needs 62 bags of 80 lb concrete with 10% waste, or 56 bags without any allowance. The slab holds 33.33 cubic feet, which is 1.23 cubic yards — 1.36 cubic yards with waste. The same slab needs 82 bags of 60 lb mix, 98 bags of 50 lb mix, or 123 bags of 40 lb mix with waste. That is about 4,960 lbs of dry mix to move, so a 10×10 slab is already a big bag job: read the ready-mix comparison below before you buy. Run your own numbers through the Concrete Slab Calculator, and use the concrete guide if your slab is not 10×10.

The walkthrough: a 10x10 patio slab, step by step

Follow the steps in order and every number below is one you can check on your own slab. The slab is a 10 ft × 10 ft patio, 4 in thick, in QUIKRETE Concrete Mix, with a 10% waste allowance.

Step 1: measure and square the form. Measure 10 ft by 10 ft, then check the diagonals: both should read the same, about 14.14 ft (the square root of 10² + 10²). If the diagonals differ, the form is a parallelogram, not a square, and the volume is wrong before you start.

Step 2: work out the volume. Volume = length × width × thickness in feet. Thickness in feet is thickness in inches divided by 12, so 4 in is 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft. Volume = 10 × 10 × 0.333 = 33.33 cubic feet. Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27, so 33.33 ÷ 27 = 1.23 cubic yards.

Step 3: add 10% waste. Spillage, an uneven base and a form that bulges a little all eat concrete, so multiply by 1.10: 33.33 × 1.10 = 36.67 cubic feet, or 1.36 cubic yards. Order by this figure, not the neat one — the pour does not stop at the neat volume.

Step 4: divide by the bag yield. An 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cubic feet, so 36.67 ÷ 0.60 = 61.1, rounded up to 62 bags. A 60 lb bag yields 0.45 cubic feet, so 36.67 ÷ 0.45 = 81.5, rounded up to 82 bags. Always round up: partial bags cannot be bought, and running short mid-pour is the classic expensive mistake.

Step 5: count the water. An 80 lb bag starts with 6 pints of water and takes up to 9 pints at most, so 62 bags need 62 × 6 = 372 pints, which is 46.5 gallons to start, and up to 558 pints (about 69.75 gallons) at the maximum. Have the full amount on site, but add it slowly and aim for a 2 to 3 in slump.

Step 6: count the gravel base. A 4 in compacted gravel base under the slab is the same volume as the slab itself: 10 × 10 × (4 ÷ 12) = 33.33 cubic feet, or 1.23 cubic yards of base gravel. Size it with the Gravel Patio Calculator before you order, and compact it before the pour.

Step 7: decide bags or truck. Bags cost about $310 (62 bags at about $5 each). Ready-mix costs about $204 for 1.36 cubic yards at about $150 a yard, plus a $125 to $200 short-load delivery fee, so $329 to $404 in total. The totals are close — the real difference is labour: 62 bags is nearly 2.5 tons to mix, haul, screed and finish before it sets. If the truck can reach the form, the truck is the easier pour at this size.

Step 8: pour day. Buy one spare bag beyond the 62 if the pour cannot pause, check the order weight against your vehicle (4,960 lbs of dry mix), and have help lined up — a 10×10 slab at 4 in is a full day's mixing for a small crew. Set control joints while the concrete is still workable, and cure it before it dries out.

Bags vs ready-mix: where the crossover sits

Compare both options per cubic foot, using your own local quotes. Bags: cost per cubic foot = bag price ÷ yield per bag, such as $5 ÷ 0.60 = $8.33 per cubic foot for an 80 lb bag. Ready-mix: cost per cubic foot = delivered price per cubic yard ÷ 27, plus any short-load fee, such as $150 ÷ 27 = $5.56 per cubic foot before the fee.

As a rule of thumb, below about 1 cubic yard (about 45 bags of 80 lb mix) bags usually win, because the truck's short-load fee is spread over too little concrete. Between about 1 and 1.5 cubic yards — exactly where a 10×10 slab at 4 in sits — the two options cost about the same, and the choice is labour and access: can the truck reach the form, and do you have the crew to mix 62 bags before the first batch starts to set? Past about 1.5 to 2 cubic yards the truck almost always wins on both cost and your back, even after paying the short-load fee. Bagged concrete can still be cheaper for access-limited jobs where a truck cannot reach, but the labour is real: at 62 bags you are mixing, hauling and placing nearly 2.5 tons by hand.

Water and mixing: what 62 bags actually needs

For QUIKRETE Concrete Mix, an 80 lb bag starts with 6 pints of water and has a maximum of 9 pints. A 60 lb bag runs 4 to 7 pints and a 40 lb bag runs 3 to 4.5 pints. The target slump is 2 to 3 in. For this slab, 62 bags of 80 lb mix need 372 pints (46.5 gallons) of water to start, and up to 558 pints (about 69.75 gallons) at the maximum — have it all on site before the first bag is opened.

Start at the lower figure and add water until the mix is workable, then check the slump if you have a cone. Never add water just to make the pour easier: extra water weakens the finished slab. Mix in small batches if your mixer only holds one or two bags at a time, and keep the batches consistent — a slab poured from batches mixed at different water levels cures unevenly and can crack along the batch lines.

Mistakes that change the bag count

The common errors are the same ones in the common concrete mistakes guide, and each one moves the count by a lot. Rounding down: 61.1 bags truncated to 61 leaves the pour 0.07 cubic feet short — always round up. Skipping the allowance: the count drops from 62 bags to 56, and the last corner of the slab goes unpoured. Entering inches as feet: a 4 in slab typed as 4 ft gives 734 bags instead of 62 — convert inches to feet first. Using one yield for every size: dividing a 60 lb order by 0.60 gives 62 bags, but the 60 lb yield is 0.45, which gives 82. Forgetting the base: the gravel base is a separate order of 1.23 cubic yards, and it has to be compacted before the pour, not after. Ordering the exact count with no spare: buy one extra bag if the pour cannot pause, because a short pour costs more than one bag.

Method and limits

This guide counts bags by slab volume (length × width × thickness in feet, divided by 27 for cubic yards), multiplied by 1.10 for a 10% waste allowance, divided by the yield per bag (80 lb 0.60, 60 lb 0.45, 50 lb 0.375, 40 lb 0.30 cubic feet), with every count rounded up to whole bags. The yields are the QUIKRETE Concrete Mix 1101 data sheet figures (revised October 2022) as used by the calculator guides this page was checked against, and the 10% waste default follows the Sakrete concrete calculators. The cost figures (about $5 per 80 lb bag, about $150 per cubic yard delivered, $125 to $200 short-load fee) are planning figures from published 2026 guides, not quotes — check your local store and ready-mix supplier before you buy. Bag yields apply to QUIKRETE Concrete Mix; other brands and mixes differ, so check the yield printed on the exact bag you buy. Thickness guidance is common residential practice: 4 in for patios and walkways, 5 to 6 in for driveways and heavy loads. Structural slabs, slabs over about 4 ft tall walls, and anything carrying a vehicle need proper design — check with your local building department. Treat the total as a planning estimate and confirm the final order with your supplier before you buy.
