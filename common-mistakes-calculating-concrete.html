Common Mistakes When Calculating How Much Concrete You Need

Seven small errors that leave a truck short or order twelve times too much — with the correct numbers for a real driveway slab, so you can check your own figure before you call the supplier.

Short answer

The most expensive mistake is entering the thickness in inches as if it were feet: a 20 ft × 12 ft slab 4 in thick needs 2.96 cubic yards, but entered as 4 ft it comes out as 35.56 cubic yards — 12 times too much. Done right, that slab is 240 sq ft × 4/12 ft = 80 cu ft, which is 2.96 cubic yards, 3.26 cubic yards with a 10% waste allowance, or 147 bags of 80 lb concrete. The other costly mistakes are forgetting the waste allowance, measuring outside the forms, leaving out a section of the pour, and trusting a bag yield from memory. The formula

Volume in cubic feet = length (ft) × width (ft) × thickness (ft). Thickness in inches must be divided by 12 first: 4 in is 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft. Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27. Cubic yards to order = cubic yards × 1.10 for a 10% waste allowance. Bags = cubic feet with waste ÷ bag yield (an 80 lb bag yields 0.6 cu ft, a 60 lb bag yields 0.45 cu ft, a 40 lb bag yields 0.3 cu ft). For a round post hole or form tube, volume = π × radius² × depth, all in feet. Add every section of the pour in cubic feet first, then convert to cubic yards once at the end.

Worked example: 20 ft × 12 ft driveway slab, 4 in thick

Step 1: the area is 20 × 12 = 240 sq ft. Step 2: the thickness is 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft. Step 3: the volume is 240 × 0.333 = 80 cu ft. Step 4: in cubic yards that is 80 ÷ 27 = 2.96 cubic yards. Step 5: with 10% waste, 80 × 1.10 = 88 cu ft, and 88 ÷ 27 = 3.26 cubic yards — order 3.3 cubic yards from the supplier. Step 6: in bags, 88 ÷ 0.6 = 146.7, rounded up to 147 bags of 80 lb, or 88 ÷ 0.45 = 195.6, rounded up to 196 bags of 60 lb. One cubic yard is 45 bags of 80 lb, so a quick check is 3.26 × 45 = 146.7 bags — the same 147. If your own calculation is far from these numbers for a slab this size, one of the seven mistakes below is almost certainly in it.

Mistake 1: entering 4 inches as 4 feet

This is the single biggest unit mistake, and it is also the easiest to make. Length and width are in feet, so the thickness looks like it belongs in the same units — but 4 in is 0.333 ft, not 4 ft. For our slab the wrong version is 20 × 12 × 4 = 960 cu ft, which is 960 ÷ 27 = 35.56 cubic yards, against the correct 2.96. That is 12 times too much concrete, and 12 times the price. The fix: always divide the thickness in inches by 12 before you multiply, and if a result looks wrong by a large factor, the thickness field is the first place to look.

Mistake 2: confusing area with volume

240 square feet is not 240 cubic feet. Area alone says nothing about how much concrete you need until the thickness is in the calculation — a 240 sq ft slab at 4 in needs 2.96 cubic yards, the same area at 6 in needs 4.44 cubic yards, and at 8 in it needs 5.93. Each extra inch on this slab adds 0.74 cubic yards, about 25% per inch at 4 in. The fix: never order from a square-footage figure; multiply by the thickness in feet first, then divide by 27.

Mistake 3: measuring outside the forms

Concrete fills the space inside the forms, not the outside. Measuring the outside of the formwork adds the thickness of the form boards — typically 1.5 in on each side — to both dimensions, which inflates the volume by roughly 5% on a small slab and more on a narrow one. Worse, forms are rarely perfectly parallel, so a single measurement can be off in either direction. The fix: measure to the inside of the forms, in at least two places along each dimension, and use the larger figure where they differ.

Mistake 4: forgetting a section of the pour

Most real projects have more than one part — a slab and its walkway, a driveway and its apron, a patio and its thickened edge — and the most common omission is not a mismeasurement but a forgotten section. A 3 ft × 10 ft walkway at 4 in is 30 sq ft × 0.333 ft = 10 cu ft, which is 0.37 cubic yards missing from the order, and a thickened edge around a 20 × 12 ft slab adds more. The fix: write a list of every section, calculate each in cubic feet, add them all, and only then convert to cubic yards. Converting each section separately and adding rounded results compounds the rounding error.

Mistake 5: ordering the bare figure with no waste allowance

The calculated volume assumes exact dimensions and a perfectly flat base, and neither holds on a real site. Subgrades dip, forms bow slightly under pressure, trenches over-dig, and some concrete is always lost to spillage and to what stays in the chute or mixer. For our slab the waste allowance is 88 − 80 = 8 cu ft, or 0.30 cubic yards — order without it and the pour comes up short by that much, leaving a cold joint where the fresh concrete meets the part that has already started to set. The fix: add about 10% for a normal pour, 5% only on a clean, well-formed pour, and more than 10% on rough ground or irregular forms. A second delivery and a short-load fee cost far more than the extra concrete.

Mistake 6: trusting a bag yield from memory

One bag is not one cubic foot. An 80 lb bag yields 0.6 cu ft, a 60 lb bag yields 0.45 cu ft, and a 40 lb bag yields 0.3 cu ft — and the yield differs by product, so a figure remembered from a different mix is wrong. For our slab, dividing the 88 cu ft with waste by 1 cu ft per bag gives 88 bags, but the real count is 147 bags of 80 lb — 59 bags short. The fix: read the yield printed on the bag you are actually buying, divide the cubic feet by that yield, and round up. One cubic yard is 45 bags of 80 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb, or 90 bags of 40 lb.

Mistake 7: using the diameter instead of the radius for round holes

Post holes and form tubes are cylinders, so the volume is π × radius² × depth — and volume grows with the square of the radius, so a small diameter error has an outsized effect. A 12 in diameter hole 3 ft deep has a radius of 0.5 ft, so the volume is 3.1416 × 0.5² × 3 = 2.36 cu ft, about 4 bags of 80 lb. Put the diameter in place of the radius and the same formula gives 3.1416 × 1² × 3 = 9.42 cu ft — four times too much. The fix: halve the diameter to get the radius before you square it, and use the Concrete Footing Calculator for footings and the Concrete Driveway Calculator for a full driveway pour.

Method and limits

This guide catches calculation mistakes for rectangular slabs, footings and round post holes: volume is length × width × thickness in feet, divided by 27 for cubic yards, plus a 10% waste allowance, with bag counts from the yield printed on the bag. It assumes one thickness across the slab, measurements taken inside the forms, and a 4 in slab for the table. Thickened edges, haunches, steps and grade beams need their own volume added as separate sections, and irregular outlines should be split into rectangles and triangles rather than averaged into one rectangle. Thickness itself is a specification from your plan, designer or local code — this guide checks the arithmetic, it does not choose the thickness. Treat the total as a planning estimate and confirm the final order with your supplier before you pour. Frequently asked questions

How much concrete do I need for a 10x10 slab 4 inches thick?

A 10x10 ft slab 4 inches thick is 100 sq ft times 4/12 ft, which is 33.33 cubic feet or 1.23 cubic yards. With 10% waste that is 36.67 cubic feet or 1.36 cubic yards, which is 62 bags of 80 lb concrete.

How many 80 lb bags of concrete are in a cubic yard?

45 bags. One cubic yard is 27 cubic feet and one 80 lb bag yields 0.6 cubic feet, so 27 divided by 0.6 is 45 bags. A 60 lb bag yields 0.45 cubic feet, so a cubic yard needs 60 of those, and a 40 lb bag yields 0.3 cubic feet, so a cubic yard needs 90.

How much extra concrete should I order?

Order about 10% extra for a normal slab or driveway. Subgrades dip, forms bow slightly, trenches over-dig and some concrete is lost to spillage, so the bare geometric figure almost always comes up short. Use 5% only on a clean, well-formed pour, and more than 10% on rough ground or irregular forms.

What happens if I order too little concrete?

The pour stops before the form is full and you get a cold joint where the fresh concrete meets the part that has already started to set, which is a weak line in the slab. Topping up a ready-mix order means a second delivery and often a short-load fee, and bagged mix bought later may come from a different batch. Ordering a little extra is cheaper than running short.

Is 4 inches thick enough for a driveway slab?

4 inches is the usual minimum for a patio, sidewalk or a driveway that carries only passenger cars, and 5 to 6 inches is used for driveways that carry trucks or other heavy vehicles. Thickness is a specification from your plan, designer or local code, not a number to guess, and adding one inch to a slab adds 25% more concrete.

How do I calculate concrete for a round post hole?

Use pi times the radius squared times the depth, all in feet. A 12 inch diameter hole 3 ft deep has a radius of 0.5 ft, so the volume is 3.1416 times 0.25 times 3, which is 2.36 cubic feet, about 4 bags of 80 lb concrete. Use the radius, not the diameter: using the diameter in the same formula gives 9.42 cubic feet, four times too much.
