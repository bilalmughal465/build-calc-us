How Many Studs Do I Need for a Wall? 16" vs 24" Spacing Comparison Table

One formula, both spacings compared side by side, a full worked example for a 20 ft wall with a door, the extra studs for openings and corners, and the plate lumber maths — so you order once and do not run short.

Short answer

A 20 ft wall at 16 in on-center needs 16 field studs; with 1 door opening and 10% waste, buy 22 studs, and allow 60 ft of plate lumber. The quick rule is studs = wall length in inches ÷ spacing, rounded up, plus 1 for the stud at the far end. Spacing makes a big difference: the same 20 ft wall needs only 11 field studs at 24 in on-center instead of 16. The formula: length, spacing, plus one

Studs = ceil(wall length in inches ÷ spacing) + 1. The +1 is the stud at the far end of the wall — the spacing formula counts the gaps between studs, so without it you would be one stud short at the end. Spacing is measured on-center, from the centre of one stud to the centre of the next, not from edge to edge. A 20 ft wall is 240 in long, so at 16 in on-center: 240 ÷ 16 = 15, plus 1 = 16 field studs. At 24 in on-center: 240 ÷ 24 = 10, plus 1 = 11 field studs. Field studs are the regular studs along the wall — openings, corners and waste are added after, as the worked example shows.

Worked example: a 20 ft wall, 16" spacing, 1 door

Step 1: measure the wall — 20 ft long, 16 in on-center spacing, one standard door opening, no corners counted yet. Step 2: convert the length — 20 ft × 12 = 240 in. Step 3: field studs — 240 ÷ 16 = 15, plus 1 for the end stud = 16 field studs. Step 4: the door opening — add 4 studs (2 king studs + 2 jack studs) = 16 + 4 = 20 studs. Step 5: waste — 20 × 1.10 = 22 studs to buy. Step 6: plates — one bottom plate and two top plates, so 3 runs × 20 ft = 60 ft of plate lumber. Step 7: cost planning — at editable US estimates of $4 per stud and $2 per foot of plate lumber, 22 × $4 = $88 for studs and 60 × $2 = $120 for plates, a total of $208. Treat these prices as planning estimates, not quotes — lumber prices vary by region, species and season, so confirm with your supplier before you buy.

16" vs 24" spacing: which one to use

16 in on-center is the standard spacing for load-bearing walls and exterior walls, and it is the spacing most building codes expect. It gives the wall more strength, supports heavier loads, and holds drywall and siding firmly with less flex between studs. 24 in on-center uses fewer studs and less lumber, but it is only used for some non-load-bearing interior walls, where the wall carries no weight from above. A 24 in spaced wall can feel less solid and may need thicker drywall to stop it flexing. The spacing is not a free choice: check your local building code before you frame, because the code decides what is allowed for your wall type, and an inspector can ask for 16 in spacing even on a wall you thought was non-load-bearing. Openings: doors and windows need extra studs

Every opening breaks the regular stud spacing and needs its own framing. A door opening needs 4 extra studs: 2 king studs that run the full height of the wall on either side of the opening, and 2 jack studs that sit under the header and carry its weight down to the floor. A window opening needs 6 extra studs: the same 2 king studs and 2 jack studs as a door, plus 2 cripple studs — short studs that fill in the wall above and below the window, because a window leaves wall to fill on both sides of the opening. These are extra to the field studs from the table, so always add them after the spacing count, not instead of it. King studs are full-height studs that also come from your stud stock, so this guide counts all 4 (2 king + 2 jack) per door opening; guides that count only the 2 jack studs will show a lower total. The header above each opening is a separate piece of lumber and is not counted in the stud total — size it to your local code, because the header carries the load over the opening.

Corners: 2 extra studs each

Add 2 extra studs per corner where two walls meet. A corner needs more than a single stud: the extra studs give the next wall something solid to nail to and give the drywall a backing edge in the corner. A room with four corners needs 8 extra studs on top of the field studs, openings and waste — on a small room that is a large share of the order, so count corners before you buy. If you are framing a single straight wall with no corners, this does not apply.

Waste: add 10%

Add 10% waste to the total stud count before you buy, and round up to whole studs. Lumber is a natural material: some studs arrive bowed, twisted or split, some get cut short by a measuring mistake, and every opening adds offcuts that are too short to reuse. On the worked example, 20 studs × 1.10 = 22 studs to buy — the extra 2 cover the bad pieces. On a long wall with many openings, 10% is a floor, not a ceiling: if the lumber at your yard looks rough, allow a little more rather than a little less, because a return trip for two studs costs more than the studs.

Common mistakes that leave you short

The classic errors are the same ones in the other framing and quantity guides: measuring the wrong thing, then trusting the maths. The most common mistakes are forgetting the +1 end stud (the spacing formula counts gaps, not studs), forgetting the king and jack studs around doors and windows, and measuring edge-to-edge instead of on-center — edge-to-edge spacing packs the studs too close together and over-orders, while forgetting the end stud under-orders. Other common slips are forgetting the corner studs, forgetting the cripple studs above and below windows, counting the plate lumber inside the stud total (plates are separate), and ordering exactly the field-stud count with no waste allowance. Measure the wall, count the field studs, add openings and corners, then add 10% waste — in that order.

Method and limits

This guide counts studs by the formula studs = ceil(wall length in inches ÷ spacing) + 1, with spacing measured on-center at 16 in or 24 in. Openings add 4 studs per door (2 king + 2 jack) and 6 per window (2 king + 2 jack + 2 cripple studs); corners add 2 studs each; the total is then increased by 10% for waste and rounded up to whole studs. Plate lumber is counted separately at 3 runs of the wall length (one bottom plate and two top plates). The cost example uses editable US estimates of $4 per stud and $2 per foot of plate lumber — prices vary by region, species and season, so confirm with your supplier. This formula counts lumber only: it does not size headers, spans or loads, and it does not check your local building code — verify the spacing, header sizes and wall type with your local code before you frame. Frequently asked questions

How many studs do I need for a 12 ft wall?

A 12 ft wall needs 10 field studs at 16 in on-center (144 in ÷ 16 = 9, plus 1 for the end stud), or 7 studs at 24 in on-center. Add 4 studs per door opening, 6 per window, 2 per corner, and 10% waste on the total before you buy.

How many studs do I need for an 8 ft wall?

An 8 ft wall needs 7 field studs at 16 in on-center (96 in ÷ 16 = 6, plus 1 for the end stud), or 5 studs at 24 in on-center. Corners, door and window openings, and 10% waste are extra — a plain 8 ft wall with no openings and no corner needs 8 studs to buy at 16 in spacing.

Should I space studs 16 or 24 inches apart?

Use 16 in on-center for load-bearing walls and exterior walls — it is the standard spacing and the one most building codes expect. 24 in on-center is only used for some non-load-bearing interior walls, where it saves lumber and still supports drywall. Always check your local building code before framing, because the code, not this guide, decides what is allowed.

How many studs do I need for a door opening?

Add 4 studs per door opening: 2 king studs that run full height on either side of the opening, and 2 jack studs that sit under the header and carry it. These are extra to the field studs counted by the spacing formula, so a 20 ft wall with one door needs 16 field studs plus 4 opening studs, or 20 studs before waste.

How many studs do I need for a window opening?

Add 6 studs per window opening: 2 king studs and 2 jack studs, the same as a door, plus 2 cripple studs — short studs that fill in above and below the window. A window needs more framing than a door because the wall must be filled in both above and below the opening.

How much plate lumber do I need for a wall?

Count 3 runs of the wall length: one bottom plate and two top plates (a double top plate). A 20 ft wall needs 3 × 20 ft = 60 ft of plate lumber. Buy it in the longest lengths you can carry, and remember the plates are separate from the studs — the stud count in this guide does not include plate lumber.
