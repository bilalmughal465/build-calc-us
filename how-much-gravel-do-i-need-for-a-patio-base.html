How Much Gravel Do I Need for a Patio Base? #57 Stone vs Crusher Run Comparison Table

One patio base worked from measuring to ordering — the volume and tonnage for every common patio size, which stone to buy for your soil, and the compaction allowance that leaves most people a wheelbarrow short.

Short answer

A 12 ft × 12 ft patio with a 4 in compacted base needs 2.04 cubic yards of gravel to order — about 2.8 tons of #57 stone, or 3.1 tons of crusher run — with a 15% compaction allowance. The finished volume is only 1.78 cubic yards; the extra 15% is the loose stone that packs down when you compact it. Add 1 in of bedding sand on top: 0.44 cubic yards, about 0.6 tons, or 24 bags of 50 lb sand. Work the steps below for your own size, or run your numbers through the Gravel Patio Calculator.

The worked example: a 12 ft × 12 ft patio base, step by step

Two calculators do the two jobs in this project: the Gravel Patio Calculator for the base stone, and the Paver Calculator for the pavers that sit on it. Here is the whole job by hand.

Step 1: Measure the patio. Measure the length and width in feet, then add about 6 in on each side — the base should extend past the pavers so the edge row has something solid to sit on. Our example is a plain 12 ft × 12 ft square, 144 sq ft. If the patio is L-shaped, split it into rectangles, work each one separately, and add the volumes.

Step 2: Pick the depth for your soil. 4 in is the minimum base depth for a patio on well-drained soil, under the CMHA standard (formerly ICPI Tech Spec 2). Average soil takes 4 to 6 in; clay or wet soil takes 6 to 10 in; cold climates with freeze-thaw cycles add 2 to 4 in more. Our example is 4 in on average, well-drained ground. The depth table below shows what each soil needs.

Step 3: Work out the finished volume. Volume = length × width × depth in feet, and depth in feet is depth in inches ÷ 12. For our patio: 12 × 12 × (4 ÷ 12) = 144 × 0.33 = 48.00 cu ft. Divide by 27 for cubic yards: 48.00 ÷ 27 = 1.78 cu yd. That is the volume of the finished, compacted base — not what you order.

Step 4: Add the compaction allowance, then round up. Loose gravel loses 15% to 25% of its depth when compacted, because the stones lock together and the fines fill the voids. For a hand-tamped patio base, 15% is the standard allowance: 1.78 × 1.15 = 2.04 cu yd to order. Order for the finished depth alone and your 4 in base finishes at about 3 1/2 in — thin enough to settle under the pavers within a season. Never round down — a part yard still has to be ordered whole.

Step 5: Convert cubic yards to tons. Gravel is sold by the ton, so multiply by the stone's weight per cubic yard. #57 stone weighs about 2,700 lb per cubic yard (1.35 tons); crusher run weighs about 3,000 lb per cubic yard (1.50 tons). For our patio: 2.04 × 1.35 = 2.75, so about 2.8 tons of #57 stone; 2.04 × 1.50 = 3.06, so about 3.1 tons of crusher run. The same volume weighs more as crusher run because the dust fills the gaps between the stones.

Step 6: Work out the bedding sand. The pavers sit on 1 in of coarse concrete sand (ASTM C33) screeded level over the compacted base. For our patio: 12 × 12 × (1 ÷ 12) = 12.00 cu ft, or 0.44 cu yd. Sand weighs about 2,700 lb per cubic yard, so 0.44 × 1.35 = about 0.6 tons, or 12.00 ÷ 0.5 = 24 bags of 50 lb sand at 0.5 cu ft per bag. Sand is the bedding layer, not a substitute for the base — sand alone settles and fails.

Step 7: Compare bulk against bagged. The same 2.04 cubic yards as bagged 50 lb gravel (0.5 cu ft per bag) is 2.04 × 27 = 55.08 cu ft, divided by 0.5 = 110.2, so 111 bags — and at big-box prices bagged stone costs many times more per ton than bulk delivery. For any patio over about 1 ton, bulk delivery from a landscape supply yard is the practical choice: at a typical US estimate of $30 to $60 per ton for #57 stone, 2.8 tons costs about $84 to $168 for the stone, plus $50 to $150 delivery. Confirm prices with your local supplier before you order.

Check your soil before you dig: if the ground stays wet days after rain, treat it as clay and go to the thick end of the range. On clay or weak subgrade, lay woven geotextile fabric between the soil and the stone — without it, fine particles migrate up into the aggregate over two to three seasons and the base loses its drainage. The compaction step most calculators skip

The single biggest reason people come up short on gravel is ordering for the finished depth and forgetting that loose stone packs down. When you spread loose gravel and run a plate compactor over it, the stones lock together and the fines fill the voids, and the layer loses 15% to 25% of its depth. The allowance depends on the job: 15% for a hand-tamped patio base, 20% for a driveway under a plate compactor, and 25% for crusher run under a roller, where the fines migrate and lock tightest. Decorative stone that is never compacted needs no allowance at all. Compact in lifts of 2 to 3 in, not all at once — a plate compactor only densifies the top few inches, so dumping 6 in and running one pass leaves the lower half loose, exactly where you need density. Compact the exposed subgrade before adding any stone, too: soft subgrade defeats all the compaction work on the aggregate above it.

Common mistakes that leave you short

The classic errors are the same ones in the other gravel guides: ordering for the finished depth without the compaction allowance, mixing up cubic yards and tons (they are not interchangeable — the factor differs by stone type), and using rounded pea gravel or river stone for a base that can never lock up. Other common slips are dumping the full depth in one layer instead of compacting in 2 to 3 in lifts, skipping geotextile fabric on clay and then watching the stone sink into the mud, setting the drainage slope by eye instead of measuring 1/4 in per foot away from the house, and forgetting the bedding sand entirely and then having no sand on site when the pavers arrive. Measure the patio, pick the depth for your soil, work the volume, add the compaction allowance, convert to tons with the right stone's weight — in that order.

Method and limits

This guide calculates gravel volume as length × width × depth, with depth converted from inches to feet by dividing by 12 and cubic feet converted to cubic yards by dividing by 27. A 15% compaction allowance is added for a hand-tamped patio base before rounding up to the nearest tenth of a ton. Stone weights are the mid-range planning figures: #57 stone about 2,700 lb per cubic yard (1.35 tons), crusher run about 3,000 lb per cubic yard (1.50 tons), pea gravel about 2,900 lb per cubic yard (1.45 tons), and bedding sand about 2,700 lb per cubic yard. Published densities vary — some guides put #57 stone as low as 1.21 tons per cubic yard — so confirm the density with your supplier before you order by weight. Bagged stone is 50 lb bags at 0.5 cu ft per bag. Depth guidance follows the CMHA standard (formerly ICPI Tech Spec 2): 4 in minimum for patios on well-drained soil, 6 to 10 in on clay or wet soil, plus 2 to 4 in in cold climates, with 1 in of ASTM C33 bedding sand. The cost example uses typical US estimates of $30 to $60 per ton for #57 stone and $50 to $150 per load for delivery — prices vary by region, supplier and haul distance, so confirm with your supplier. This guide counts base stone and bedding sand only: it does not size the pavers, choose the edge restraint, or set the depth — depth, drainage and subgrade preparation are set by your soil, climate and local code, so verify them with your contractor before you dig. Frequently asked questions

How much gravel do I need for a 12x12 patio base?

A 12 ft x 12 ft patio with a 4 in compacted base needs 1.78 cubic yards of gravel as finished volume, or 2.04 cubic yards to order with a 15% compaction allowance — about 2.8 tons of #57 stone (2,700 lb per cubic yard) or 3.1 tons of crusher run (3,000 lb per cubic yard). Add 1 in of bedding sand on top: 0.44 cubic yards, about 0.6 tons, or 24 bags of 50 lb sand.

How deep should a patio gravel base be?

4 in is the minimum for a patio on well-drained soil, under the CMHA (formerly ICPI) standard. Go to 4 to 6 in on average soil and 6 to 10 in on clay or wet soil, and add 2 to 4 in more in cold climates with freeze-thaw cycles. Add 1 in of ASTM C33 bedding sand on top of the base. A 4 in base with 1 in of sand and a 2 3/8 in paver means digging about 7 1/3 in below the finished height.

Should I use #57 stone or crusher run for a patio base?

Crusher run is the usual pick under pavers: its stone dust fills the voids and it packs to a near-solid platform, which is why Belgard specifies it for residential bases. #57 stone is the pick where drainage matters — it is clean, angular 3/4 to 1 in stone with no fines, so water drains freely through it, but it never locks up on its own. Never use rounded pea gravel or river stone for a base: the stones roll like ball bearings and cannot interlock under compaction.

How many tons of gravel are in a cubic yard?

About 1.35 tons per cubic yard for #57 stone or crushed limestone (2,700 lb), about 1.45 tons for pea gravel (2,900 lb), and about 1.50 tons for crusher run or river rock (3,000 lb). Published figures vary by source and supplier — some guides put #57 as low as 1.21 tons per cubic yard — so confirm the density with your supplier before you order by weight.

How much gravel do I need for a 10x10 patio base?

A 10 ft x 10 ft patio with a 4 in compacted base needs 1.23 cubic yards as finished volume, or 1.42 cubic yards to order with a 15% compaction allowance — about 2.0 tons of #57 stone or 2.2 tons of crusher run. Its volume is 100 x (4 divided by 12) = 33.33 cu ft, divided by 27 = 1.23 cu yd, times 1.15 = 1.42 cu yd to order.

Do I need sand under pavers?

Yes — 1 in of coarse concrete sand (ASTM C33) screeded level on top of the compacted gravel base. It is the bedding layer the pavers sit in, not a substitute for the base: sand alone settles and fails. A 12 ft x 12 ft patio needs 0.44 cubic yards of sand (12.00 cu ft), about 0.6 tons, or 24 bags of 50 lb sand at 0.5 cu ft per bag.
