How Many Concrete Blocks Do I Need for a Wall? A Worked Walkthrough With a Block Table

Two ways to count blocks, a full worked example for a 20 ft x 8 ft wall, a table for every common wall size, and the mortar and pallet maths — so you order once and do not run short.

Short answer

A 20 ft × 8 ft wall needs 180 standard 8×8×16 concrete blocks, or 198 blocks with 10% waste. That is 12 courses of 15 blocks each. Plan for about 6 bags of 80 lb mortar and about 3 pallets (at 90 blocks per pallet). The quick rule is 1.125 blocks per square foot of wall, but count by courses for the final order — the course method catches part courses the shortcut misses. Two ways to count: area shortcut and course count

Both methods use the same standard block, an 8×8×16 CMU (concrete masonry unit). Pick the course-count method for the order you actually place, and use the area shortcut for a fast estimate.

The area shortcut: 1.125 blocks per square foot

One standard block covers 0.889 sq ft of wall face (8 in × 16 in = 128 sq in, and 128 ÷ 144 = 0.889). So blocks = wall area in sq ft × 1.125, then add 10% waste. For the 20 ft × 8 ft wall: 160 sq ft × 1.125 = 180 blocks, and 180 × 1.10 = 198 blocks with waste. It is fast, and it is right whenever the wall divides neatly into whole blocks.

The course count: rows first, then blocks per row

Courses = wall height in inches ÷ 8, rounded up. Blocks per course = wall length in inches ÷ 16, rounded up. Multiply the two, then add 10% waste. For the same wall: 96 in ÷ 8 = 12 courses, and 240 in ÷ 16 = 15 blocks per course, so 12 × 15 = 180 blocks, 198 with waste. This is the method to trust, because it rounds up each course separately — a wall that is 10 ft 6 in long needs 8 blocks per course, not 7.875, and the shortcut quietly drops that half block.

Nominal vs actual size: the 3/8 in mortar joint

An 8×8×16 block is the nominal size — the size on the label. In your hand it measures 7-5/8 × 7-5/8 × 15-5/8 in. The missing 3/8 in on each face is the standard mortar joint: once the block is laid with mortar on top and on one end, the installed size is the full 8 in by 16 in. Always use nominal sizes in your calculation. Using the actual 15-5/8 in length instead of 16 in double-counts the mortar joints and inflates your count by 2 to 3% — about 5 extra blocks on a 198-block order. The same mistake is the most common error in the common concrete mistakes guide: measuring the wrong thing, then trusting the maths.

Worked example: a 20 ft x 8 ft garden wall

Step 1: measure the wall — 20 ft long, 8 ft high, no openings, standard 8×8×16 blocks. Step 2: count the courses — 8 ft is 96 in, and 96 ÷ 8 = 12 courses. Step 3: count blocks per course — 20 ft is 240 in, and 240 ÷ 16 = 15 blocks per course. Step 4: multiply — 12 × 15 = 180 blocks. Step 5: check with the area shortcut — 20 × 8 = 160 sq ft, and 160 × 1.125 = 180 blocks, so both methods agree. Step 6: add 10% waste — 180 × 1.10 = 198 blocks to order. Step 7: mortar — about 1 bag of 80 lb mortar per 33 blocks, so 198 ÷ 33 = 6 bags. Step 8: pallets — at 90 blocks per pallet, 198 ÷ 90 = 2.2, so order 3 pallets, or 2 full pallets (180 blocks) plus 18 loose blocks if your supplier sells loose. Step 9: cost planning — blocks typically run $1.55 to $3.40 each at US retail, varying by region and supplier, so 198 blocks is roughly $307 to $673 for blocks alone, before mortar, delivery or labour. Confirm the price with your supplier; treat the range as a planning figure, not a quote.

Second example: a small 10 ft x 4 ft garden wall

Courses: 4 ft is 48 in, and 48 ÷ 8 = 6 courses. Blocks per course: 10 ft is 120 in, and 120 ÷ 16 = 7.5, rounded up to 8 blocks per course. Base count: 6 × 8 = 48 blocks. With 10% waste: 48 × 1.10 = 52.8, so order 53 blocks. Mortar: 53 ÷ 33 = 1.6, so buy 2 bags of 80 lb mortar. Notice the course method gives 48 while the area shortcut gives 40 × 1.125 = 45 — the 3-block gap is the half block per course (7.5 rounded up to 8) that the shortcut ignores. On a small wall that gap is the difference between finishing the last course and running short.

Deducting door and window openings

Measure every opening, multiply width by height for its area, add the opening areas together, and subtract from the gross wall area before you divide by 0.889. A standard 3 ft × 7 ft door is 21 sq ft, which saves 21 × 1.125 = 23.6, about 24 blocks. A standard 3 ft × 4 ft window is 12 sq ft, saving 12 × 1.125 = 13.5, about 14 blocks. A wall with two doors and three windows needs about 90 fewer blocks — skipping the deduction is how an order comes in a pallet too heavy. Do not deduct the opening until you have measured it: a door with a lintel course above it still needs the blocks over the opening, and the lintel itself is a separate material.

Half blocks and part courses

Real walls rarely divide into whole blocks. A wall 10 ft 6 in long needs 8 blocks per course: 7 full blocks and 1 half block, cut from a full block. A wall 8 ft 4 in high needs 13 courses: 12 full courses and 1 part course of 4 in blocks, cut down from full blocks. Count every part course as a full course of blocks — the cut pieces come from full blocks you have already bought, and the waste factor covers the offcuts. Buy a few half blocks ready-made if your supplier stocks them; they save cutting time at corners and around openings. If your wall is L-shaped or U-shaped, split it into straight sections, count each section by courses, and add the sections together — do not count the corner twice.

Mortar, pallets and ordering

Mortar: about 1 bag of 80 lb mortar per 33 standard blocks, so 6 bags for the 198-block wall and 2 bags for the 53-block garden wall. Pallets: most US masonry yards pack about 90 blocks per pallet, though some pack 108 or 120 — confirm with your supplier. Order by the full pallet where you can: spare blocks are far cheaper than a second delivery, and a shortfall near the end of the job costs a day. For a retaining wall, the block count works the same way, but the material list does not stop at blocks and mortar: walls over 4 ft tall usually need a structural engineer’s review, plus a reinforced footing, vertical rebar, cell grout and drainage aggregate. Method and limits

This guide counts standard 8×8×16 CMU blocks by courses (wall height in inches ÷ 8, rounded up; wall length in inches ÷ 16, rounded up) and cross-checks with the area method (wall area × 1.125 blocks per sq ft, face area 0.889 sq ft per block), with 10% added for waste and every count rounded up to whole blocks. Mortar is estimated at about 1 bag of 80 lb per 33 blocks and pallets at about 90 blocks each — confirm both with your supplier, because mortar use varies with joint thickness and pallet counts vary by yard. Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the number of concrete blocks for a wall?

Multiply wall length by wall height to get the gross area in square feet, subtract door and window openings to get the net area, then divide by 0.889 sq ft (the face area of one standard 8x8x16 block) and add 10% waste. Or count by courses: courses = wall height in inches divided by 8, rounded up; blocks per course = wall length in inches divided by 16, rounded up; multiply the two and add 10% waste. A 20 ft x 8 ft wall is 180 blocks by either method, 198 with waste.

How many concrete blocks do I need for a 20x8 wall?

A 20 ft x 8 ft wall needs 180 standard 8x8x16 blocks, or 198 blocks with 10% waste. That is 12 courses of 15 blocks each. Plan for about 6 bags of 80 lb mortar (about 1 bag per 33 blocks) and about 3 pallets if your supplier packs 90 blocks per pallet. If the wall has a door or window, subtract those openings first: a standard 3 ft x 7 ft door saves about 24 blocks.

How many blocks are in a pallet of concrete blocks?

A standard pallet of 8x8x16 concrete blocks holds about 90 blocks at most US masonry yards, though some suppliers pack 108 or 120 per pallet. Always confirm the pallet count with your supplier before you order, because it changes how many pallets to buy: 198 blocks is 3 pallets at 90 per pallet (2 full pallets plus 18 loose blocks).

How much mortar do I need for a concrete block wall?

Plan for about 1 bag of 80 lb mortar per 33 standard 8x8x16 blocks. A 20 ft x 8 ft wall (198 blocks with waste) needs about 6 bags. A small 10 ft x 4 ft garden wall (53 blocks with waste) needs about 2 bags. Mortar use varies with joint thickness and block type, so check the bag and confirm the final count with your supplier.

What is the difference between nominal and actual concrete block size?

Nominal size is the size printed on the label, 8x8x16 in, and it includes the 3/8 in mortar joint. Actual size is what you measure in your hand: 7-5/8 x 7-5/8 x 15-5/8 in. The mortar joints make up the missing 3/8 in on each face once the block is laid. Always use nominal sizes in your calculation, because they already account for the joints; using actual sizes double-counts the joints and inflates your block count.

How many concrete blocks do I need per square foot of wall?

About 1.125 blocks per square foot of wall before waste, for a standard 8x8x16 block with a 3/8 in mortar joint. That is 113 blocks per 100 sq ft, or about 124 blocks with 10% waste. Use this shortcut for a quick estimate, but count by courses for the final order, because the course method rounds up each course and catches part courses the shortcut misses.
