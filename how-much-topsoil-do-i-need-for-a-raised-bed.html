How Much Topsoil Do I Need for a Raised Bed? A Worked Project Walkthrough

Two 4x8 ft beds worked from tape measure to bags to cost — the volume formula, the 10% settling allowance everyone forgets, the soil for every common bed size, the depth each crop actually needs, and the bags-vs-bulk crossover, with every number shown so you can check it against your own beds.

Short answer

Two 4 ft × 8 ft raised beds, 12 in deep, need 70.4 cubic feet of soil to order — 2.61 cubic yards, or 47 bags of 1.5 cu ft — with a 10% settling allowance. The beds hold 64 cubic feet net, and fresh soil sinks as it is watered in, so ordering the net volume leaves both beds a couple of inches low by midsummer. In bags that is about $211.50 at the example price of $4.50 a bag; in bulk it is about $167.45 including delivery at the example prices below. Work the steps for your own beds, or size the order with the Raised Bed Soil Calculator. The full home and garden calculators list covers the topsoil, the compost and the rest of the garden job.

The worked project: two 4 ft × 8 ft beds, 12 in deep, step by step

Step 1 — Measure the inside, not the outside. Measure length and width inside the boards, because the soil fills the inside. Two beds of 4 ft × 8 ft, each 32 sq ft, give 64 sq ft in total. If your boards are 2 in thick, the outside footprint overstates the soil by about a tenth — measure inside.

Step 2 — Net volume. Volume = length × width × depth, with the depth in feet. 12 in is 1 ft, so each bed holds 4 × 8 × 1 = 32 cu ft, and two beds hold 64 cu ft net.

Step 3 — Add 10% for settling. Fresh soil mix sinks 10% to 20% in its first season as it gets watered in. At 10%: 64 × 1.10 = 70.4 cu ft to order. A bed filled exactly to the rim without this allowance ends up a couple of inches low by midsummer.

Step 4 — Convert to cubic yards. Bulk soil is sold by the cubic yard, and one cubic yard is 27 cubic feet. 70.4 ÷ 27 = 2.61 yd³.

Step 5 — Convert to bags. Bags are sold by cubic feet, so divide by the bag size and round up — you cannot buy part of a bag. 70.4 ÷ 1.5 = 46.93, so 47 bags of 1.5 cu ft. The same volume is 71 bags of 1 cu ft (70.4 ÷ 1 = 70.4, rounded up) or 36 bags of 2 cu ft (70.4 ÷ 2 = 35.2, rounded up).

Step 6 — Weigh it. Topsoil weighs about 69 lb per cubic foot, so 70.4 × 69 = 4,858 lb, about 2.43 tons. That is the number that decides whether the car can carry it or the bulk truck has to — 47 bags at roughly 100 lb each is not a one-trip car load.

Step 7 — Split the mix. A common raised-bed blend is 60% topsoil, 30% compost and 10% aeration material. On 70.4 cu ft that is 42.24 cu ft of topsoil, 21.12 cu ft of compost and 7.04 cu ft of aeration material such as coarse perlite or pine bark fines. If you buy a ready-made raised bed mix, skip the split and order the total.

Step 8 — Price it both ways. In bags: 47 × $4.50 = $211.50. In bulk: 2.61 yd³ × $45 = $117.45, plus about $50 delivery = $167.45. Bulk saves about $44.05 on this project — but only because the order is over 2 cubic yards. For one small bed the delivery charge alone costs more than the soil, and bags win. Cross-check the single-bed figure: one 4 × 8 bed at 12 in deep is 32 cu ft net, 35.2 with settling, 24 bags of 1.5 cu ft — the same numbers the soil calculators other sites publish, so the arithmetic holds. How deep the soil should be for what you grow

4 to 6 in: lettuce, radishes, spinach and other shallow-rooted greens.

8 to 12 in: most vegetables — beans, cucumbers, peppers and herbs. This is the most common range for raised beds.

12 to 18 in: tomatoes, carrots, potatoes and other deep-rooted crops.

Over 18 in: only worth it for deep root vegetables or a bed built tall for easy reaching — and then only the top 12 in needs to be good mix.

Bags vs bulk: where the crossover sits

Bags are sold by cubic feet and bulk by the cubic yard, and the crossover is about one cubic yard. Below it, the delivery charge eats the bulk saving; above it, bulk pulls ahead fast. On the two-bed project above, bags cost $211.50 and bulk with delivery costs $167.45 — a $44.05 saving. On a single 4 × 4 bed (17.6 cu ft, 12 bags), bags cost about $54.00 at $4.50 a bag, while the same soil in bulk is about $29.38 plus $50 delivery — about $79.38, so bags win by about $25.38. The rule is simple: under about a yard, buy bags; over a yard, get a bulk quote with the delivery included.

What changes the answer for topsoil

Your depth. Depth is the biggest single lever after the footprint. A 4 × 8 bed at 6 in needs 17.6 cu ft with settling and the same bed at 24 in needs 70.4 — four times as much — so measure the fill depth you actually want, not the board height.

Your settling allowance. Fresh mix sinks 10% to 20% in its first season. Ordering the net volume leaves the bed low by midsummer, and topping up later costs a second trip. 10% is the usual allowance; fluffy compost-heavy mixes can need 15% to 20%.

Your bed shape. A round or half-barrel bed uses the area of a circle — π × radius² × depth — not length × width. A 4 ft diameter round bed 12 in deep holds 12.57 cu ft net (3.14 × 2² × 1), 13.8 with settling, 10 bags of 1.5 cu ft.

Your mix. Straight topsoil is the cheapest fill, but vegetables grow better in the 60/30/10 blend of topsoil, compost and aeration material. The blend costs more per cubic foot than topsoil alone, so price the mix, not just the topsoil. The Garden Soil Calculator sizes the blend for a garden bed the same way.

Your mulch on top. A 2 to 3 in mulch layer on top of the soil is separate from the soil order — it keeps moisture in and weeds down. Size it with the mulch guide: a 4 × 8 bed at 3 in needs about 8 cu ft of mulch, 4 bags of 2 cu ft.

Common mistakes that leave a bed short

Measuring the outside of the bed. The soil fills the inside. On a 4 × 8 bed with 2 in boards, outside measurements overstate the volume by about a tenth — and on a small bed that is a whole bag.

Entering inches as feet. 12 inches is 1 foot, not 12 feet. Entering the depth as 12 in the feet box orders twelve times too much soil — the same unit mistake that ruins concrete orders.

Skipping the settling allowance. Fresh soil sinks as it is watered in. Ordering the exact net volume leaves the bed a couple of inches low by midsummer, and the top-up is a second trip and a second delivery charge.

Buying bags without checking the bag size. Bags come in 1, 1.5 and 2 cu ft, and some are sold by quarts — a 50-quart bag is about 1.94 cu ft. 47 bags of 1.5 cu ft is not the same order as 47 bags of 1 cu ft, so read the bag before you count.

Rounding down. Soil is sold whole. 46.93 bags is an order for 47, not 46 — and the spare bag is the one you reach for when the bed settles and needs topping up.

Method and limits

This guide calculates topsoil as volume = length × width × depth, with depth converted from inches to feet by dividing by 12, then cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27 and bags = cubic feet with the settling allowance ÷ bag size, rounded up — the same formula the BuildCalc Raised Bed Soil Calculator runs. The settling allowance is 10% unless stated otherwise; fresh mix can settle 10% to 20% in its first season. Topsoil weight uses about 69 lb per cubic foot, so one cubic yard is about 0.93 tons — wet soil weighs more, so check the bag label or your supplier's figure. The $4.50 per bag, $45 per cubic yard and $50 delivery figures are the guide's example prices, not quoted market prices, and prices vary a lot by region and supplier. The 60/30/10 mix is a common raised-bed blend, not a soil test result — your soil and your crops may call for a different blend. This guide counts soil volume only: it does not price the bed boards, hardware, mulch, plants, seeds, watering, delivery distance, or labour, and it does not cover your local soil quality or any contamination in fill soil. Frequently asked questions

How much soil do I need for a 4x8 raised bed?

A 4 ft by 8 ft bed filled 12 inches deep holds 32 cubic feet of soil. Add 10% for settling and you need 35.2 cubic feet to order — 1.30 cubic yards, or 24 bags of 1.5 cubic feet. Two beds need double: 70.4 cubic feet, 2.61 cubic yards, 47 bags.

How many bags of topsoil do I need for a raised bed?

Divide the cubic feet you need, including the 10% settling allowance, by the bag size and round up, because you cannot buy part of a bag. A single 4x8 bed at 12 inches deep needs 35.2 cubic feet, so 24 bags of 1.5 cubic feet, 36 bags of 1 cubic foot, or 18 bags of 2 cubic feet — check the bag size printed on the bag, because sizes vary.

How deep should soil be in a raised bed?

Most vegetables grow well in 8 to 12 inches of soil, and 12 inches is the standard fill depth for a raised bed. Shallow greens manage in 4 to 6 inches, while tomatoes and root vegetables want 12 to 18 inches. For a deep bed you do not need good soil all the way down — fill the bottom with logs and branches and keep the top 10 to 12 inches for the mix.

Is it cheaper to buy topsoil in bags or in bulk?

Bulk is usually cheaper once you need about a cubic yard or more. Two 4x8 beds need 47 bags of 1.5 cubic feet, about $211.50 at the example price of $4.50 a bag, against about $117.45 for 2.61 cubic yards in bulk at $45 a cubic yard plus about $50 delivery — about $167.45 in total. Bags win for one small bed, because delivery alone costs more than the soil.

How much does topsoil weigh?

Topsoil weighs about 69 lb per cubic foot, so the 70.4 cubic feet for two 4x8 beds weighs about 4,858 lb, or 2.43 tons. The same soil weighs more when it is wet, so check the bag label or your supplier's figure before you plan the haul — a full cubic yard is about 0.93 tons.

What is the best soil mix for a raised bed?

A common blend is 60% topsoil, 30% compost and 10% aeration material such as coarse perlite or pine bark fines. For two 4x8 beds that is 42.24 cubic feet of topsoil, 21.12 of compost and 7.04 of aeration material. If you buy a ready-made raised bed mix, ignore the split and order the total volume.
