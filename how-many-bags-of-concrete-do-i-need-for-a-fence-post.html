How Many Bags of Concrete Do I Need for a Fence Post? A Checklist With a Worked Example

Seven checks, one bag chart for every common post, and a worked example for a whole 100 ft fence — so you buy the right number of bags the first time.

Short answer

A 4×4 post in a 10 in × 30 in hole with 6 in of gravel at the bottom needs about 0.92 cubic feet of concrete: 2 bags of 80 lb mix, 3 bags of 60 lb mix, or 3 bags of 50 lb fast-setting mix. A 6×6 post in a 16 in × 36 in hole needs about 2.97 cubic feet: 5 bags of 80 lb. For a whole fence, multiply the per-post count by the number of posts and add 10% waste — a 100 ft fence with 14 posts needs 31 bags of 80 lb with waste. Work through the checklist below, or read your count straight off the chart. Try the Concrete Slab Calculator for slabs and the Concrete Footing Calculator for footings — a post hole is a different job from both.

The checklist: seven checks before you buy

Run through these in order. Each one changes the bag count, so skipping one is how a fence run comes up short.

1. Size the hole from the post, not from a guess

Hole diameter is about 3 times the post width: 10 to 12 in for a 4×4 post, which is actually 3.5 in square, and 16 to 18 in for a 6×6 post, which is actually 5.5 in square. Hole depth is about one-third of the fence height above ground plus 6 in of gravel, and at least 24 in deep in total — a 6 ft fence needs a 30 in hole, an 8 ft fence a 36 to 38 in hole. Gate and corner posts carry more load, so use the larger hole size there. In cold climates, take the hole below the local frost line so frost heave does not lift the post; your building department can tell you the depth.

2. Call 811 before you dig

Call 811 (in the US) before you dig so buried utility lines are marked. It is free, and hitting a line costs far more than a day of waiting. Dig the hole slightly wider at the bottom than the top, so the concrete footing resists being pulled up.

3. Put 6 in of gravel at the bottom

Add about 6 in of gravel and tamp it before the post goes in, so water drains away from the end grain of wood posts. The gravel is not counted as concrete — the concrete height is the hole depth minus the gravel depth. Forgetting the gravel layer is the most common reason a count comes out high.

4. Work out the concrete volume for one hole

Concrete per post = hole volume minus the post, over the concrete height only. Hole volume = 3.1416 × (diameter ÷ 2)² × concrete height, in cubic inches, and the post is its width × thickness × concrete height (or 3.1416 × radius² for a round steel post). Divide by 1,728 to get cubic feet. Then divide by the bag yield and round up — you cannot split a bag between holes cleanly, and each hole is usually mixed on its own.

Bag yields are the standard figures printed on US bagged concrete (QUIKRETE/Sakrete data sheets). The number worth memorising is 45 bags of 80 lb per cubic yard: one cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, and 27 ÷ 0.60 = 45.

5. Pick the bag size for the job

Fast-setting 50 lb bags are poured dry into the hole and set in about 20 to 40 minutes, which is ideal for fence posts — but each bag fills less volume, so you buy more of them. Regular 60 or 80 lb mix is mixed in a tub or wheelbarrow first, takes several hours to set, and costs less per cubic foot, which suits gate posts and long fence runs. Never use mortar mix for a post: it has no coarse aggregate and is far weaker than concrete mix.

6. Round up per post, then add 10% for the fence

Round the bag count up for each post first — a hole that needs 1.53 bags takes 2 bags, not 1.5. Then multiply by the number of posts and add 10% for spillage, uneven hole depths and mixing variations. Count the posts properly: a 100 ft fence with posts every 8 ft has 14 posts (13 gaps plus the end post), not 12 or 13.

7. Check the ready-mix line

Once the total passes about half a cubic yard — roughly 23 bags of 80 lb, or about 12 posts at 2 bags each — get a ready-mix quote before you buy bags. Bagged mix runs roughly $250 to $350 per cubic yard once you count every bag, against about $140 to $180 per cubic yard delivered for ready-mix, though short-load fees apply under about 3 yards. A long fence run is also a full day of mixing by hand, while a truck delivers one uniform batch.

Two things to read from the chart. First, hole width matters more than most people expect: a 12 in hole takes about half again as much concrete as a 10 in hole at the same depth (1.40 against 0.92 cubic feet), because the volume grows with the square of the diameter. Second, the 50 lb fast-setting column is always the highest — the convenience of dry-pouring costs you extra bags, so for a long run the 80 lb bags are usually the cheaper buy.

Worked example: a 100 ft fence, 14 posts

Step 1: the fence is 100 ft long with posts every 8 ft, so it has 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5 gaps, rounded up to 13 panels and 14 posts including both end posts. Step 2: each post is a 4×4 in a 10 in × 30 in hole with 6 in of gravel, so the concrete height is 30 − 6 = 24 in. Step 3: hole volume is 3.1416 × 5² × 24 = 1,885 cubic inches; the post below grade is 3.5 × 3.5 × 24 = 294 cubic inches. Step 4: concrete per post is (1,885 − 294) ÷ 1,728 = 0.92 cubic feet. Step 5: in 80 lb bags, 0.92 ÷ 0.60 = 1.53, rounded up to 2 bags per post, 28 bags for 14 posts; in 50 lb fast-setting, 0.92 ÷ 0.375 = 2.45, rounded up to 3 bags per post, 42 bags for 14 posts. Step 6: add 10% waste — 28 × 1.10 = 30.8, so buy 31 bags of 80 lb (or 42 × 1.10 = 46.2, so 47 bags of 50 lb). Step 7: the whole fence is 14 × 0.92 = 12.89 cubic feet, or 0.48 cubic yards — just under the half-yard ready-mix line, so bags are still the sensible buy. Step 8: gravel for the bottom of each hole is 3.1416 × 5² × 6 = 471 cubic inches, or 0.27 cubic feet per hole, 3.82 cubic feet for all 14 holes — about 8 bags of 0.5 cubic feet. If your own fence is 20 posts instead of 14, the volume is 18.4 cubic feet (0.68 cubic yards, about 31 bags before per-post rounding, 40 after it) — past the ready-mix line, so get a quote.

Follow the set and cure times on the bag. Wait before hanging gates or attaching heavy panels — a post that moves while the concrete is green will lean for the life of the fence. Set the post, brace it plumb in two directions, fill with concrete to just below ground, then slope (crown) the top away from the post so water runs off. Once the posts are set, use the fence pickets guide and the Fence Picket Calculator for the pickets, and the concrete guide for any slab or footing on the same job.

Method and limits

This guide counts bagged concrete for fence post holes: hole volume as a cylinder (3.1416 × radius² × concrete height) minus the post below grade, over a 6 in gravel base, divided by the printed bag yield (0.60 cubic feet for 80 lb, 0.45 for 60 lb, 0.375 for 50 lb fast-setting) and rounded up per post, with 10% added for the whole fence. It assumes the hole sizes in the chart, one bag size across the fence, and measurements taken as dug. Hole sizes are common US installer guidelines; local codes, frost depth and wind loads can require larger or deeper holes, and a building department's requirement overrides anything here. Bag yields are typical values printed on US bagged concrete; check the bag you buy. Ready-mix prices are typical US planning ranges, not quotes — prices vary by region, supplier and season. Treat the total as a planning estimate and confirm the final order with your supplier before you buy. Frequently asked questions

How many bags of concrete do I need per fence post?

A 4x4 post in a 10 in x 30 in hole with 6 in of gravel needs about 0.92 cubic feet of concrete: 2 bags of 80 lb mix, 3 bags of 60 lb mix, or 3 bags of 50 lb fast-setting mix. A 6x6 post in a 16 in x 36 in hole needs about 2.97 cubic feet: 5 bags of 80 lb, 7 of 60 lb, or 8 of 50 lb fast-setting. Always round up per post, because each hole is mixed on its own.

How deep should a fence post hole be?

About one-third of the fence height above ground, plus about 6 in of gravel at the bottom, and at least 24 in deep in total. A 6 ft fence needs about 24 in of post in the ground plus 6 in of gravel, so a 30 in hole; an 8 ft fence needs about 32 in of post plus gravel, so a 36 to 38 in hole. In cold climates, go below the local frost line so frost heave does not lift the post.

How wide should a fence post hole be?

About 3 times the post width: a 10 to 12 in hole for a 4x4 post (which is actually 3.5 in square) and a 16 to 18 in hole for a 6x6 post (actually 5.5 in square). Gate and corner posts carry more load, so use the larger hole size there. A wider hole uses much more concrete: a 12 in hole takes about half again as much concrete as a 10 in hole at the same depth.

How many 50 lb bags of fast-setting concrete do I need per fence post?

About 3 bags of 50 lb fast-setting mix for a 4x4 post in a 10 in x 30 in hole, and about 8 bags for a 6x6 post in a 16 in x 36 in hole. Each 50 lb bag yields about 0.375 cubic feet, less than an 80 lb bag at 0.60 cubic feet, so fast-setting always takes more bags for the same hole. Pour it dry into the hole and add water as the bag directs; it sets in about 20 to 40 minutes.

Can I pour concrete dry into a fence post hole?

Yes, for fence posts, with fast-setting mix: pour the dry mix into the hole around the braced post, then add water as the bag directs. It is a widely accepted method for posts and mailboxes. For slabs and footings, mix the concrete first — dry-pouring a slab gives an uneven mix and a weak result. Use the Concrete Footing Calculator for footings, which are a different job from a post hole.

How many bags of concrete do I need for a whole fence?

Count the posts first, then multiply the per-post count and add 10% waste. A 100 ft fence with posts every 8 ft has 14 posts. At 2 bags of 80 lb per 4x4 post, that is 28 bags, and 31 bags with 10% waste. In 50 lb fast-setting mix the same fence is 42 bags, 47 with waste. The worked example in this guide shows every step, including the gravel for the bottom of each hole.
