How Many Shingles Do I Need for a Roof? A Checklist With a Worked Example

One roof worked from footprint to bundles to cost — the 10 checks that stop you ordering a row short, the pitch table, the bundles for every common roof size, and the starter, cap and underlayment most calculators leave out, with every number shown so you can check it against your own roof.

Short answer

A 28 ft × 36 ft house at a 6/12 pitch needs 38 bundles of field shingles — 42 bundles in total with starter and ridge cap, about $1,470 at $35 per bundle — with a 10% waste allowance. The roof surface is 1,127 sq ft, which is 12.40 squares with waste, and standard shingles ship 3 bundles to the square. Work the checklist below for your own roof, or size the order with the Shingle Bundle Calculator. The checklist: 10 checks before you order

1. Measure the footprint, overhangs included. Measure the house length and width eave to eave, not wall to wall — the roof is bigger than the walls by the overhang on every side. Add 1 to 2 ft per side if you only have the wall dimensions. Split dormers, additions and garage wings off and measure each as its own little roof, then add them in.

2. Find the pitch. Pitch is written as rise over a 12 in run — 6/12 means the roof rises 6 in for every 12 in across. Measure it from the attic with a level and a tape, or read it off the plans. If you guess the pitch, the whole order is wrong: a 4/12 roof and a 12/12 roof on the same footprint differ by a third in surface.

3. Apply the pitch multiplier. A sloped roof has more surface than its flat footprint. Multiply the footprint by the multiplier for your pitch (table below): roof surface = footprint × multiplier. The multiplier is exact geometry — the square root of 1 plus (rise ÷ 12) squared.

4. Add waste — 10% for a simple gable, 15% for a hip or cut-up roof. Waste is the diagonal cuts at hips and valleys, the starter course along the eaves, and damaged pieces. A simple gable with no hips, valleys or dormers takes 10%; a hip roof or one with dormers and valleys takes 15%; a very cut-up roof can need 18 to 20%.

5. Convert to squares. Roofers sell by the square — 100 sq ft of roof surface. Squares = roof surface with waste ÷ 100.

6. Convert to bundles — 3 per square, rounded up. Standard 3-tab and most architectural shingles ship 3 bundles to the square, so each bundle covers about 33.3 sq ft. Always round up: a half bundle cannot be bought, and the leftover covers the next repair. Heavy designer lines can take 4 or more bundles per square — check the wrapper.

7. Add the starter course. Starter strip runs along the eaves and usually up the rakes, under the first course. Purpose-made starter covers about 105 linear feet per bundle, so divide your total eave-plus-rake length by 105 and round up. Cutting 3-tab shingles into starter gives only about 78 linear feet per bundle.

8. Add ridge cap. Cap shingles cover the ridge and any hips. Purpose-made cap covers about 25 linear feet per bundle, so divide ridge plus hip length by 25 and round up. A bundle of 3-tab shingles cut into caps covers about 33 linear feet.

9. Add underlayment. One roll of synthetic underlayment covers roughly 10 squares. Divide your squares by 10 and round up — and add ice-and-water shield at the eaves and valleys if your climate or code calls for it.

10. Price it and order. Multiply total bundles by your local price per bundle, then confirm the order against the wrapper coverage of the exact shingle you are buying. Prices vary a lot by region and by shingle line, so use your supplier's price, not a national average.

The worked example: a 28 ft × 36 ft house, 6/12 gable, step by step

Step 1 — Footprint. 28 × 36 = 1,008 sq ft, overhangs included.

Step 2 — Roof surface. The 6/12 multiplier is 1.118, so 1,008 × 1.118 = 1,127.0 sq ft of shingled surface.

Step 3 — Waste. A simple gable takes 10%: 1,127.0 × 1.10 = 1,239.7 sq ft to cover.

Step 4 — Squares. 1,239.7 ÷ 100 = 12.40 squares.

Step 5 — Field bundles. 12.40 × 3 = 37.19, so order 38 bundles of field shingles. That is about 806 architectural shingles (12.40 squares × 65 shingles per square) and about 3,224 nails at 4 per shingle.

Step 6 — Starter. Eaves: 2 × 36 = 72 ft. Rakes: 2 × 28 × 1.118 = 62.6 ft. Total 134.6 ft ÷ 105 ft per bundle = 1.28, so 2 starter bundles.

Step 7 — Ridge cap. The ridge runs the 36 ft length: 36 ÷ 25 = 1.44, so 2 cap bundles.

Step 8 — Underlayment. 12.40 squares ÷ 10 squares per roll = 1.24, so 2 rolls.

Step 9 — Price it. At the calculator's $35 per bundle, 38 field bundles cost 38 × $35 = $1,330; starter and cap add 4 × $35 = $140, so 42 bundles in total cost $1,470 before underlayment, nails and delivery. Cross-check: a 2,000 sq ft footprint at the same pitch and waste works out to 74 bundles by the same steps, which matches the worked examples other roofing calculators publish — so the arithmetic holds. What changes the answer for shingles

Your pitch. Pitch is the biggest single lever after footprint. Going from 4/12 to 12/12 on the same house adds a third more surface — and a third more bundles — so measure it rather than guessing.

Your shingle type. Standard 3-tab and most architectural shingles are 3 bundles per square, but heavy designer lines can be 4 or 5. A 12.40-square roof needs 38 bundles at 3 per square and 50 at 4 per square — a $420 difference at $35 a bundle. The wrapper states the coverage; that number governs.

Your roof shape. Hips, valleys, dormers and skylights all eat shingles in diagonal cuts. That is why the waste allowance jumps from 10% to 15% — and why a hip roof of the same footprint costs more to shingle than a simple gable.

Metal instead of shingles. If you are weighing a metal roof against shingles, the area maths is the same — squares from footprint and pitch — but the material is sold by the panel, not the bundle. Compare the two with the Metal Roof Calculator before you commit.

Underlayment and ventilation. The bundles are only part of the order. Underlayment runs about 10 squares per roll, and ridge vents, drip edge and flashing are sold by the linear foot — the Roof Underlayment Calculator sizes the felt and the Roofing Calculator builds the full list.

Common mistakes that leave a roof short

Measuring wall to wall instead of eave to eave. The roof is bigger than the house by the overhang on every side. Forgetting 1 ft of overhang on a 28 × 36 ft house loses about 140 sq ft of surface — 5 bundles.

Using the footprint as the roof area. A pitched roof always has more surface than its footprint — 11.8% more at 6/12, 41.4% more at 12/12. Ordering by footprint alone guarantees a short order on any roof steeper than flat.

Skipping the waste allowance. The cuts at hips, valleys and rakes are real shingles. A 10% allowance on a simple gable is the minimum; ordering the exact square footage leaves you a row short on the last slope.

Forgetting starter and cap. The field bundles do not cover the first course or the ridge. On the worked example that is 4 extra bundles — leaving them out means a second trip to the supplier mid-job.

Rounding down. Bundles are sold whole. 37.19 bundles is an order for 38, not 37 — and the spare bundle is the one you reach for when a shingle cracks or a storm lifts a tab next year.

Method and limits

This guide calculates shingles as squares = footprint × pitch multiplier × (1 + waste) ÷ 100, with the pitch multiplier = √(1 + (rise ÷ 12)²), then bundles = squares × 3, rounded up — the same formula the BuildCalc Shingle Bundle Calculator runs. Standard coverage is 3 bundles per square (about 33.3 sq ft per bundle) for 3-tab and most architectural shingles, with about 65 architectural shingles or 80 three-tab shingles per square and 4 nails per shingle; purpose-made starter covers about 105 linear feet per bundle, purpose-made cap about 25 linear feet per bundle, and one synthetic underlayment roll about 10 squares — all wrapper-stated figures that vary by product, so check the wrapper of the shingle you buy. The $35 per bundle price is the calculator's default example price, not a quoted market price, and prices vary a lot by region and shingle line. This guide counts shingles, starter, cap and underlayment only: it does not price flashing, drip edge, vents, ice-and-water shield, nails, delivery, labour, tear-off of the old roof, or a permit, and it does not cover your local building code or your shingle maker's installation guidance. Frequently asked questions

How many bundles of shingles do I need for a 1,500 sq ft roof?

A 1,500 sq ft footprint at a 6/12 pitch is 1,677 sq ft of roof surface; with 10% waste that is 18.45 squares, so 56 bundles at 3 bundles per square, plus starter and ridge cap on top. If 1,500 sq ft is already the roof surface rather than the footprint, skip the pitch step: 1,500 times 1.10 is 1,650 sq ft, 16.5 squares, 50 bundles.

How many shingles are in a bundle?

About 21 architectural shingles per bundle (64 shingles per square divided by 3 bundles) or about 27 three-tab shingles per bundle (80 per square divided by 3). The count follows from the shingle size and exposure, and the bundle wrapper states the coverage — you buy bundles, not individual shingles, so the piece count matters mainly for nails.

How many bundles of shingles are in a square?

Three bundles per square for standard 3-tab shingles and most architectural (laminated) shingles — one square is 100 sq ft of roof surface, so each bundle covers about 33.3 sq ft. Heavy designer and premium lines can take 4 or more bundles per square because each bundle covers less; the wrapper states the coverage, so check it before you order.

How much waste should I add when ordering shingles?

Add 10% for a simple gable roof with no hips, valleys or dormers, and 15% for a hip roof or a roof with dormers, valleys or skylights. Waste is not padding — it is the diagonal cuts at hips and valleys, the starter course along the eaves, and damaged pieces. A very cut-up roof with many dormers and intersecting planes can need 18 to 20%.

How many squares is my roof?

Squares are roof surface divided by 100. First find the roof surface: footprint (including overhangs) times the pitch multiplier — 1.118 for a 6/12 pitch, 1.054 for 4/12, 1.202 for 8/12. A 28 ft by 36 ft footprint at 6/12 is 1,008 times 1.118 = 1,127 sq ft of roof surface, or 11.27 squares before waste.

How many ridge cap shingles do I need?

Divide the ridge length by the cap coverage — about 25 linear feet per bundle of purpose-made cap shingles. A 36 ft ridge needs 36 divided by 25 = 1.44, so 2 bundles. Add hip lengths to the ridge length on a hip roof. A bundle of 3-tab shingles cut into caps covers about 33 linear feet, so check the wrapper of the cap you buy.
