How Many Cords of Firewood Do I Need for Winter? Firewood vs Propane vs Electric Comparison Table

One home worked from heat need to cords to cost — the cords for a 1,500 sq ft house, what the same winter costs in propane, oil, gas and electricity, and the checks that stop you ordering a third short, with every number shown so you can check it against your own home.

Short answer

A 1,500 sq ft home heated mainly by wood needs 3.57 cords of firewood for the winter — order 4 cords, about $1,200 at $300 per cord — with a 10% buffer. The season heat need is about 50 million BTU of delivered heat, and one cord of seasoned hardwood holds about 20 million BTU, of which a 70% efficient stove delivers about 14 million BTU to the room. At the same winter's US average prices, that wood costs about $17 per million BTU delivered, against about $34 for propane and about $53 for electric resistance heat — so wood is roughly half the cost of propane and a third the cost of baseboard electric. Work the steps below for your own home, or size your stack with the Firewood Calculator. The full construction calculators list covers the fireplace, the stove and the rest of the heating job.

The worked example: a 1,500 sq ft home, step by step

Step 1 — Fix the season heat need. A medium, average-insulation home needs about 50 million BTU (MMBtu) of delivered heat over a winter. The best way to find your own number is from last winter's fuel bills: gallons of oil or propane times their BTU content times your furnace efficiency, divided by your heated area, gives your BTU per sq ft. A tight, well-insulated house in a mild winter can run near 15,000 BTU per sq ft for the season; an older house in a cold region often runs two or three times that.

Step 2 — Work the useful heat in one cord. A cord of seasoned hardwood holds about 20 million BTU — that is the common planning figure. A stove does not deliver all of it to the room: at 70% efficiency, the useful heat is 20 × 0.70 = 14 million BTU per cord. (The EPA's figure for a typical non-catalytic stove is about 71%, so 70% is a fair example.)

Step 3 — Divide the need by the useful heat. Cords = 50 ÷ 14 = 3.57 cords exact. That is the wood you must burn, not the wood you must buy.

Step 4 — Add the buffer. A cold snap, a wetter-than-expected load or a stove run harder than planned all eat wood. Add 10%: 3.57 × 1.10 = 3.93 cords to order. Firewood is sold by the cord and the half cord, so order 4 cords.

Step 5 — Price it. At an example $300 per cord, 4 × $300 = $1,200 for the winter. At the BuildCalc calculator's $250 default, the same 4 cords cost $1,000 — cord prices vary a lot by region and species, so use your local price. That is the fuel only: cutting, splitting and stacking your own wood takes the cord price toward zero, but saws, fuel, a truck and your time all count.

Step 6 — Cross-check with the rule of thumb. Published guidance commonly suggests 2 to 3 cords per 1,000 sq ft in a cold climate. This home is 1,500 sq ft, so 1.5 × 2.5 = 3.75 cords — close to the 3.57-cord exact figure, so the arithmetic holds. If the two methods disagree by a lot, trust your fuel bills over the rule of thumb.

What changes the answer for firewood

Your cord price and species. At 70% efficiency, every $50 change in the price of a red oak cord moves the delivered cost by about $2.90 per MMBtu: $250 a cord is about $14.52 and $400 is about $23.23. A lighter species costs more per unit of heat at the same price — pine, spruce and fir hold roughly a quarter to a third less heat per cord than oak, and aspen, cottonwood and willow about a third less, so burning mostly softwood means ordering more cords.

Moisture. The heat values assume air-dried wood. Properly dried wood should read 20% or less on a moisture meter. Wet wood loses heat to boiling off water and makes more smoke and creosote — season split wood for six months to a year or more.

Stove efficiency. The 20 million BTU figure is the heat in the wood, not the heat delivered to the room. Divide by your stove's efficiency: at 70%, 3.57 cords of heat need becomes the 3.57 cords in the worked example only because the 14 million BTU useful figure already includes it. An open fireplace sends most of the heat up the chimney and needs far more wood than a stove for the same warmth.

Whether you got a full cord. A full cord is 128 cubic feet stacked. Terms such as face cord, rick and truckload have no legally defined meaning, so always ask for the stack dimensions and check them — see the trap below.

Regional prices. National averages hide big gaps. In the EIA's data for the week of November 2, 2026, propane was $2.298 a gallon across the Midwest against $3.978 in New England, and New England's residential electricity averaged 28.31¢ per kWh in August 2026 against the US average of 18.16¢. At New England's prices, propane at 90% is about $48 per MMBtu delivered, which makes firewood look much better. Look up your state rather than relying on the national figure.

The face-cord trap: how sellers short you

A face cord is one row of a full cord: 4 ft high by 8 ft long with 16 in logs. That is 4 × 8 × (16 ÷ 12) = 42.67 cubic feet — exactly one-third of a full cord. Three face cords make one full cord. The trap is paying a full-cord price for a face cord, or ordering "4 cords" and receiving 4 face cords — only 1.33 full cords, a third of what you need. Before you pay, ask for the stack dimensions, multiply length × height × log length, and divide by 128 to get the true cords — that is exactly what the Firewood Calculator does. A stack 8 ft long, 4 ft high, with 2 ft logs is 64 cubic feet, or 0.50 cords.

How much space does the wood take?

Four cords are 4 × 128 = 512 cubic feet of stacked wood. Stacked 4 ft high, that needs 512 ÷ 4 = 128 square feet of floor — about an 8 ft by 16 ft patch. Stacked 6 ft high it needs about 85 square feet. Plan the space before you order, keep the stack off the ground on pallets or rails, cover the top but leave the sides open so air moves through, and keep it away from the house wall so pests and damp do not travel in.

Common mistakes that leave a house cold

Burning green wood. Freshly cut wood holds a lot of water. Much of its heat goes into boiling that water off, and the smoke builds creosote in the chimney. Season split wood for six months to a year or more.

Buying face cords as full cords. A face cord of 16-inch logs is a third of a full cord — see the trap above. One wrong unit is the most expensive estimating error in firewood.

Ignoring stove efficiency. The heat in the wood is not the heat in the room. A 70% stove needs about 1.4 times the wood a perfect stove would, and an open fireplace far more.

Heating the whole-house area with one stove. Enter only the area the stove really reaches. A stove in a closed-off living room may heat 800 sq ft, not the whole 1,500 sq ft house — and the furnace still covers the rest.

Forgetting the buffer. Ordering the exact cordage guarantees a short winter. The 10% buffer in the worked example is the cheapest insurance in the whole job — a cord bought in January costs more than one bought in October, if you can get one at all.

Method and limits

This guide calculates firewood as season heat need ÷ useful heat per cord, with useful heat = 20 million BTU per cord of seasoned hardwood × stove efficiency, plus a 10% buffer — the same formula the BuildCalc Firewood Calculator runs for stack volume (cords = length × height × log length ÷ 128). The fuel comparison converts every fuel to dollars per million BTU of delivered heat: price ÷ (energy per unit × efficiency), using EIA conversion factors — propane 91,452 BTU per gallon, heating oil 138,500 BTU per gallon, natural gas 1,036 BTU per cubic foot, electricity 3,412 BTU per kWh — and red oak at 24.6 million BTU per cord from Utah State University Extension. Prices are the EIA's US averages for the week of November 2, 2026 (propane $2.820, heating oil $6.138 per gallon), electricity at 18.19¢ per kWh (January–August 2026 average) and natural gas at $13.96–$16.16 per Mcf (January–March 2026); the $300 cord is an example price, not a published average. Fuel prices move constantly and vary a lot by region, so use your own bill and your local cord price for the final decision — the table ranks the fuels, it does not quote them. This guide counts fuel only: it does not price the stove, the chimney, installation, a permit, delivery, or the labour of cutting, splitting and stacking, and it does not cover local burning rules or your stove maker's guidance. Frequently asked questions

How many cords of firewood do I need for a 1,500 sq ft home?

A 1,500 sq ft home heated mainly by wood, with a season heat need of about 50 million BTU, needs 3.57 cords as the exact figure, or 3.93 cords with a 10% buffer — order 4 cords. The maths is 50 million BTU divided by 14 million BTU of useful heat per cord (a 20 million BTU cord in a 70% efficient stove), times 1.10. At $300 per cord that is about $1,200 for the winter.

How many cords of firewood do I need for winter in a cold climate?

Published guidance commonly suggests 2 to 3 cords per 1,000 sq ft in a cold climate and 1 to 2 cords in a mild climate, with wood as the main heat source. For a 1,500 sq ft home that is 3 to 5 cords in a cold climate and 2 to 3 cords in a mild one; over 2,000 sq ft, expect 5 to 7 or more cords in a cold climate. These are starting points — your own fuel bills give a better number than any rule of thumb.

Is firewood cheaper than propane?

Yes, at US average prices from the EIA for the week of November 2, 2026. Propane at $2.820 per gallon in a 90% efficient furnace costs about $34.26 per million BTU of delivered heat, while red oak firewood at $300 a cord in a 70% efficient stove costs about $17.42. Wood stays cheaper until red oak costs about $590 a cord. Regional prices differ a lot — propane was $3.978 a gallon in New England against $2.298 in the Midwest the same week.

Is firewood cheaper than electric heat?

Yes, against electric resistance heat, and still cheaper than a heat pump at US average prices. Electric resistance heat at 18.19 cents per kWh costs about $53.31 per million BTU delivered, a heat pump at a COP of 2.5 costs about $21.32, and red oak firewood at $300 a cord in a 70% stove costs about $17.42. The heat pump is the closest competitor — where electricity is cheap or a heat pump runs at a high COP, the gap narrows.

What is a face cord vs a full cord?

A full cord is a stack 4 ft high, 4 ft deep and 8 ft long — 128 cubic feet of stacked wood. A face cord is one row of that stack, 4 ft high by 8 ft long with 16 in logs, about 42.67 cubic feet — exactly one-third of a full cord. Three face cords make one full cord. Terms like face cord, rick and truckload have no legally defined meaning, so always ask for the stack dimensions and check them before you pay.

How much space do 4 cords of firewood take?

Four cords are 4 times 128 = 512 cubic feet of stacked wood. Stacked 4 ft high, that needs 512 divided by 4 = 128 square feet of floor — about an 8 ft by 16 ft patch. Stacked 6 ft high it needs about 85 square feet. Plan the space before you order, keep the stack off the ground and covered on top, and leave air gaps so the wood stays dry.
