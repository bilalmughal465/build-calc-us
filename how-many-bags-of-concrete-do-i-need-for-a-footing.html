How Many Bags of Concrete Do I Need for a Footing? A Checklist With a Worked Example

One checklist, three bag tables, and a full worked example for a 24 ft strip footing — so you buy the right number of bags the first time and do not run short halfway through the pour.

Short answer

A 10 ft footing 12 in wide and 8 in deep needs 13 bags of 80 lb concrete mix with 10% waste. The footing holds 6.67 cu ft (0.25 cu yd) of concrete, and each 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cu ft — 12 bags before waste, 13 after. A round 12 in Sonotube footing 48 in deep needs 6 bags of 80 lb. Work through the checklist below for your own footing, or run your numbers through the Concrete Footing Calculator.

The checklist: 7 steps before you buy

Work the list in order — each step feeds the next, and skipping one is how people end up short.

1. Pick the footing shape. A strip footing is a long trench (length × width × depth). A round footing is a Sonotube or a dug post hole (diameter × depth). The shape decides the formula, and the two shapes are not interchangeable — a round 12 in footing holds 3.14 cu ft at 48 in deep, while a square 12 in hole the same depth holds 4 cu ft, so treating a round hole as square over-orders by about 27%.

2. Measure in feet, depth in inches. Measure the length and width in feet and the depth in inches, then convert the depth: depth in feet = depth in inches ÷ 12. An 8 in deep footing is 0.67 ft deep. This is the step where the classic mistake happens — entering 8 in as 8 ft orders twelve times too much concrete.

3. Work out the volume. Strip footing: length × width × depth in feet = cubic feet. Round footing: π × radius² × depth, with the radius in feet. Divide cubic feet by 27 if you want cubic yards. A 10 ft × 12 in × 8 in footing is 10 × 1 × 0.67 = 6.67 cu ft, or 0.25 cu yd.

4. Divide by the bag yield. An 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cu ft, a 60 lb bag about 0.45 cu ft, and a 40 lb bag about 0.30 cu ft (Quikrete and Sakrete data-sheet yields). One cubic yard takes 45 bags of 80 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb, or 90 bags of 40 lb. The 10 ft footing needs 6.67 ÷ 0.60 = 11.1, rounded up to 12 bags of 80 lb.

5. Add 10% waste, then round up. Trenches are never perfectly straight, some mix spills, and the bottom is never perfectly level. Multiply the bag count by 1.10 and round up to whole bags: 12 × 1.10 = 13.2, so 13 bags. Never round down — a part bag still has to be bought whole.

6. Multiply by the number of footings. One footing is rarely the whole job. A deck with 6 round footings needs 6 × the single-footing count: six 10 in × 48 in holes need 6 × 4 = 24 bags of 80 lb. Count the footings first, then multiply — do not guess the total from one hole.

7. Check the bags vs ready-mix crossover. Under about 1 cu yd (45 bags of 80 lb), bags are usually the cheaper and simpler choice. Above that, compare the delivered ready-mix price — a truck load avoids hours of mixing, though trucks charge a short-load fee on small loads. Worked example: a 24 ft strip footing

Step 1: the footing is 24 ft long, 12 in wide, 8 in deep, one straight run. Step 2: convert the depth — 8 in ÷ 12 = 0.67 ft. Step 3: volume — 24 × 1 × 0.67 = 16.00 cu ft, or 16 ÷ 27 = 0.59 cu yd. Step 4: bags before waste — 16.00 ÷ 0.60 = 26.7, round up to 27 bags of 80 lb. Step 5: waste — 16.00 × 1.10 = 17.60 cu ft, 17.60 ÷ 0.60 = 29.3, round up to 30 bags to buy. Step 6: other bag sizes — 60 lb bags yield 0.45 cu ft, so 17.60 ÷ 0.45 = 39.1, round up to 40 bags; 40 lb bags yield 0.30 cu ft, so 17.60 ÷ 0.30 = 58.7, round up to 59 bags. Step 7: cost planning — at an editable US estimate of $5 per 80 lb bag, 30 × $5 = $150. Step 8: the crossover check — 0.59 cu yd is well under 1 cu yd, so bags win here; a ready-mix truck would charge a short-load fee on a load this small. Treat the $5 bag price as a planning estimate, not a quote — prices vary by region and store, so confirm with your supplier before you buy.

Common mistakes that leave you short

The classic errors are the same ones in the other concrete guides: measuring the wrong thing, then trusting the maths. The most common mistakes are entering the depth in inches as if it were feet (8 in entered as 8 ft orders twelve times too much), forgetting the 10% waste allowance on a trench that is never perfectly straight, counting a round footing as a square hole (about 27% too much concrete), forgetting to multiply by the number of footings on a deck, and rounding down instead of up — a part bag still has to be bought whole. Other common slips are mixing bag sizes without changing the yield (a 60 lb bag is not two-thirds of an 80 lb bag in volume — it is 0.45 cu ft against 0.60), and forgetting that the post in a round footing displaces a little concrete, which makes the count slightly generous rather than short. Measure the footing, work the volume, divide by the yield, add waste, then multiply by the footing count — in that order.

Method and limits

This guide calculates footing volume as length × width × depth for strip footings and π × radius² × depth for round footings, with depth converted from inches to feet by dividing by 12 and cubic feet converted to cubic yards by dividing by 27. Bag yields are the manufacturer data-sheet figures: 0.60 cu ft per 80 lb bag, 0.45 per 60 lb bag, 0.30 per 40 lb bag. A 10% waste allowance is added before rounding up to whole bags. The cost example uses an editable US estimate of $5 per 80 lb bag — prices vary by region and store, so confirm with your supplier. This guide counts concrete only: it does not size the footing, choose the rebar, or set the depth — footing size and depth are set by your local building code, soil and frost line, so verify them with your local building department before you dig. Frequently asked questions

How many bags of concrete do I need for a 10 ft footing?

A 10 ft footing 12 in wide and 8 in deep needs 13 bags of 80 lb concrete mix with 10% waste. The footing holds 6.67 cu ft (0.25 cu yd) of concrete; at 0.60 cu ft per 80 lb bag that is 12 bags before waste and 13 after adding 10% and rounding up.

How many bags of concrete for a 12 inch Sonotube footing?

A 12 in diameter round footing 48 in deep needs 6 bags of 80 lb concrete mix with 10% waste. Its volume is π × (0.5 ft)² × 4 ft = 3.14 cu ft, which is 6 bags at 0.60 cu ft per bag after the waste allowance. A 10 in tube at the same depth needs 4 bags.

How do I calculate concrete for a footing?

For a strip footing, multiply length in feet by width in feet by depth in inches divided by 12 to get cubic feet, then divide by 27 for cubic yards. Divide the cubic feet by the bag yield (0.60 cu ft for an 80 lb bag, 0.45 for 60 lb, 0.30 for 40 lb), add 10% for waste, and round up to whole bags. For a round footing use π × radius² × depth instead.

How many 80 lb bags of concrete make a cubic yard?

45 bags of 80 lb concrete mix make one cubic yard, because a cubic yard is 27 cu ft and each 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cu ft (27 ÷ 0.60 = 45). A cubic yard takes 60 bags of 60 lb mix (0.45 cu ft each) or 90 bags of 40 lb mix (0.30 cu ft each), before waste.

Should I use bags or ready-mix concrete for footings?

Use bags for small footing jobs under about 1 cubic yard — a 24 ft strip footing is only 0.59 cu yd, or 30 bags of 80 lb mix. Above about 1 cubic yard (45 bags of 80 lb), ready-mix usually wins, because 45 bags cost more than a yard of ready-mix and mixing them takes hours; ready-mix trucks also charge a short-load fee on small loads, so compare the delivered price before you decide.

How deep should a concrete footing be?

A footing must sit below the local frost line, which is 12 in or less in warm southern states and 36 to 48 in across much of the northern US. The exact depth is set by your local building code and soil, not by this guide — check the frost depth and the minimum footing size with your local building department before you dig, and confirm the rebar and concrete strength they require.
