How Many Bags of Concrete Do I Need for a Driveway? A Worked Project Walkthrough

One full driveway worked from measuring to ordering — volume, bag counts for every common size, the gravel base, and the dollar crossover where bags stop making sense and a ready-mix truck takes over.

Short answer

A 12 ft × 20 ft driveway 4 in thick needs 147 bags of 80 lb concrete mix with 10% waste (134 bags before waste) — or 3.26 cubic yards of ready-mix. The honest headline is that a full driveway is a truck job, not a bag job: 147 bags cost about $735 and weigh 11,760 lb, against about $489 for the same concrete delivered. Work the walkthrough below for your own size, or run your numbers through the Concrete Driveway Calculator.

The walkthrough: a 12 ft × 20 ft driveway, step by step

Three calculators do the three jobs in this project: the Concrete Driveway Calculator for the volume and bag count, the Gravel Driveway Calculator for the base underneath, and the Concrete Cost Calculator for the bags-vs-truck decision. Here is the whole job by hand.

Step 1: Measure the driveway. Measure the length and width in feet. A single-car driveway is usually 10 to 12 ft wide; a two-car driveway is usually 20 to 24 ft wide. If the driveway is L-shaped or has a wider apron at the street, split it into rectangles, work each one separately, and add the volumes — one big rectangle over-counts the corner. Our example is a plain 12 ft × 20 ft rectangle, 240 sq ft.

Step 2: Pick the thickness. 4 in is the usual minimum for cars and light vehicles; 5 to 6 in is recommended for trucks, RVs and other heavy loads. Thickness is the number that changes your order the most — going from 4 to 5 in adds a quarter to the volume. Our example is 4 in. Check your local code before you pour; the thickness table below shows what each inch costs you.

Step 3: Work out the volume. Volume = length × width × thickness in feet, and thickness in feet is thickness in inches ÷ 12. For our driveway: 12 × 20 × (4 ÷ 12) = 240 × 0.33 = 80.00 cu ft. Divide by 27 for cubic yards: 80.00 ÷ 27 = 2.96 cu yd.

Step 4: Add 10% waste, then round up. Ground is never perfectly level, forms bulge a little, and some concrete always spills. Multiply by 1.10: 80.00 × 1.10 = 88.00 cu ft, or 88.00 ÷ 27 = 3.26 cu yd to order. Never round down — a part bag still has to be bought whole, and a part yard still has to be ordered.

Step 5: Divide by the bag yield. An 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cu ft, a 60 lb bag about 0.45 cu ft, and a 40 lb bag about 0.30 cu ft (Quikrete and Sakrete data-sheet yields). For our driveway: 88.00 ÷ 0.60 = 146.7, rounded up to 147 bags of 80 lb. Before waste it would be 80.00 ÷ 0.60 = 133.3, rounded up to 134 bags. The other sizes: 88.00 ÷ 0.45 = 195.6, so 196 bags of 60 lb; 88.00 ÷ 0.30 = 293.3, so 294 bags of 40 lb.

Step 6: Work out the gravel base. A driveway is normally poured over a compacted crushed-stone base, commonly 4 in deep with stone no larger than about three-quarters of an inch on top. For our driveway the base is the same area at 4 in: 12 × 20 × (4 ÷ 12) = 80.00 cu ft, or 2.96 cu yd. Crushed stone weighs about 2,700 lb per cu yd, so 2.96 × 2,700 = 7,992 lb, or about 4 tons. The gravel driveway walkthrough works the two-layer base in full, and the Gravel Driveway Calculator runs your own depth.

Step 7: Compare bags against ready-mix. At an editable US estimate of $5 per 80 lb bag, 147 × $5 = $735 for bags. At $150 per cu yd of ready-mix, 3.26 × $150 = $489 delivered. Bags cost $246 more, weigh 11,760 lb to move by hand, and take hours to mix — and that is before the mixer hire. The crossover is covered in full below, but the short version is: a driveway this size is a truck job.

Step 8: Check the short-load fee. Many ready-mix suppliers add a short-load fee on small orders, often below about 4 cu yd — and our 3.26 cu yd sits under that line. Ask the supplier for the delivered price including any short-load fee before you decide; even with the fee, a driveway order usually still beats 147 bags. Treat the $5 bag price and the $150 per-yard price as planning estimates, not quotes — prices vary by region and store, so confirm with your supplier before you buy.

Bags vs ready-mix: the dollar crossover

One cubic yard takes 45 bags of 80 lb, so at $5 a bag a yard of bagged concrete costs $225 before you lift a shovel — against about $150 a yard for ready-mix delivered. Bags are more expensive per yard at any size; the only question is whether the job is small enough that mixing bags is still practical. The crossover is about 1 to 1.5 cu yd: under that, a small pad or repair is a bag job; over it, the truck wins on price, time and your back. A 12 ft × 20 ft driveway is 3.26 cu yd — more than double the crossover — so bags lose on every count: $735 against $489, 11,760 lb against a truck, and a full day of mixing against an hour of pouring. The footing checklist shows the other side of the same line, where a 24 ft strip footing is only 0.59 cu yd and 30 bags win easily.

Common mistakes that leave you short

The classic errors are the same ones in the other concrete guides: measuring the wrong thing, then trusting the maths. The most common mistakes are entering the thickness in inches as if it were feet (4 in entered as 4 ft orders twelve times too much concrete), forgetting the 10% waste allowance on ground that is never perfectly level, measuring one big rectangle for an L-shaped driveway instead of splitting it into sections, forgetting the gravel base entirely and then having no stone on site, rounding down instead of up, and pricing bags without the mixer hire and the day of labour they need. Other common slips are mixing bag sizes without changing the yield (a 60 lb bag is not two-thirds of an 80 lb bag in volume — it is 0.45 cu ft against 0.60), and ordering the concrete before the forms and joints are set — saw control joints about every 8 to 10 ft so cracking follows a straight line. Measure the driveway, work the volume, divide by the yield, add waste, then compare with the truck price — in that order.

Method and limits

This guide calculates driveway volume as length × width × depth, with depth converted from inches to feet by dividing by 12 and cubic feet converted to cubic yards by dividing by 27. Bag yields are the manufacturer data-sheet figures: 0.60 cu ft per 80 lb bag, 0.45 per 60 lb bag, 0.30 per 40 lb bag. A 10% waste allowance is added before rounding up to whole bags. The gravel base estimate uses about 2,700 lb of crushed stone per cu yd. The cost example uses editable US estimates of $5 per 80 lb bag and $150 per cu yd of ready-mix — prices vary by region and store, so confirm with your supplier. This guide counts concrete and base only: it does not size the slab, choose the reinforcement, or set the thickness — thickness, reinforcement and joints are set by your local building code, soil and vehicles, so verify them with your contractor before you pour. Frequently asked questions

How many bags of concrete do I need for a driveway?

A 12 ft × 20 ft driveway 4 in thick needs 147 bags of 80 lb concrete mix with 10% waste (134 bags before waste). Its volume is 80.00 cu ft (2.96 cu yd), or 88.00 cu ft (3.26 cu yd) with the waste allowance; at 0.60 cu ft per 80 lb bag that is 147 bags. A full driveway is normally poured with ready-mix, not bags — see the cost crossover above.

How many bags of concrete for a 10x20 single-car driveway?

A 10 ft × 20 ft single-car driveway 4 in thick needs 123 bags of 80 lb concrete mix with 10% waste. Its volume is 66.67 cu ft (2.47 cu yd), or 73.33 cu ft (2.72 cu yd) with waste; 73.33 divided by 0.60 cu ft per bag is 122.2, rounded up to 123 bags.

How much concrete do I need for a driveway in cubic yards?

Multiply length in feet by width in feet by thickness in inches divided by 12 to get cubic feet, then divide by 27 for cubic yards. A 12 ft × 20 ft driveway 4 in thick is 2.96 cu yd (3.26 with 10% waste); a 20 ft × 20 ft two-car driveway at the same thickness is 4.94 cu yd (5.43 with waste).

Should I use bags or ready-mix concrete for a driveway?

Use ready-mix for a full driveway. A 12 ft × 20 ft driveway needs 147 bags of 80 lb mix, about $735 at $5 a bag, against about $489 for 3.26 cu yd of ready-mix at $150 per cu yd — and the bags weigh 11,760 lb and take hours to mix. Bags only make sense for a small pad under about 1 cu yd. Trucks may add a short-load fee on loads under about 4 cu yd, so compare the delivered price before you decide.

How thick should a concrete driveway be?

4 in is the usual minimum for cars and light vehicles; 5 to 6 in is recommended for trucks, RVs and other heavy loads. Thickness is the number that changes your order the most — going from 4 to 5 in on a 12 ft × 20 ft driveway adds 25% to the volume, from 147 bags to 184 bags of 80 lb. Your local code and soil decide the final figure, so check with your contractor.

How many 80 lb bags of concrete make a cubic yard?

45 bags of 80 lb concrete mix make one cubic yard, because a cubic yard is 27 cu ft and each 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cu ft (27 divided by 0.60 = 45). A cubic yard takes 60 bags of 60 lb mix (0.45 cu ft each) or 90 bags of 40 lb mix (0.30 cu ft each), before waste.
