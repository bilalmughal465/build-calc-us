How Much Asphalt Do I Need for a Driveway? A Checklist With a Worked Example

One driveway worked from measuring to ordering — the asphalt tonnage, the crusher-run base under it, the cost, and the checks that stop you ordering a quarter short, with every number shown so you can check it against your own driveway.

Short answer

A 20 ft × 30 ft driveway at 3 in compacted needs 11.96 tons of asphalt to order — order 12 tons, about $1,436 at $120 per ton — with a 10% waste allowance. The exact tonnage is only 10.88 tons; the extra 10% is the allowance for edges, uneven subgrade and the tail of the load. The same driveway also needs about 20 tons of crusher-run base (6 in deep), and the base often costs as much as the asphalt. Work the checklist below for your own size, or run your numbers through the Asphalt Calculator. The worked example: a 20 ft × 30 ft driveway at 3 in, step by step

Step 1 — Measure the area. 20 ft × 30 ft = 600 sq ft. For an L-shaped or wrap-around driveway, break it into rectangles and add the areas. Measure the finished surface, not the lawn around it.

Step 2 — Work the volume in cubic feet. Thickness is 3 in, so divide by 12 to get feet: 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25 ft. Volume = 600 × 0.25 = 150 cu ft, or 150 ÷ 27 = 5.56 cu yd.

Step 3 — Convert volume to tons. Compacted hot mix weighs 145 lb per cu ft, the standard estimating figure. Weight = 150 × 145 = 21,750 lb. Divide by 2,000 lb per short ton: 21,750 ÷ 2,000 = 10.88 tons exact.

Step 4 — Add the waste allowance. Asphalt is a continuous placement with little offcut, so the BuildCalc calculator uses a 10% allowance: 10.88 × 1.10 = 11.96 tons to order. Asphalt is sold by the whole ton, so order 12 tons.

Step 5 — Price it. At a typical $120 per ton, 11.96 × $120 = $1,436 in asphalt. That is the material only: the base, excavation, grading, tack coat and laying are separate, and installed cost usually runs $3 to $7 per sq ft in total — $1,800 to $4,200 for this driveway.

Step 6 — Cross-check with the trade rule. Paving crews check tonnage as 110 lb per square yard per inch of depth. This driveway is 600 ÷ 9 = 66.67 sq yd, at 3 in that is 200 yard-inches, at 110 lb each = 22,000 lb = 11.0 tons. Close to the 10.88-ton exact figure, so the arithmetic holds. (The 110 lb rule uses a slightly heavier density than 145 lb per cu ft, which is why it reads a touch high.)

The checklist: 7 checks before you order

1. Measure in rectangles. A driveway with a parking apron or a flare at the street is two or three rectangles, not one. Add each section separately — one wrong rectangle is the most common reason an order comes up short.

2. Use compacted depth, not loose depth. Hot mix compacts by roughly 25% under the roller, so 3 in finished needs about 4 in spread loose. Enter the finished depth into the calculator. If you measure the loose mat and treat it as the finished thickness, you finish about a quarter short — the single most common asphalt estimating error.

3. Add 10% waste, not 0%. Edges, thickened spots, uneven subgrade and the tail of the load all eat material. The calculator default is 10%. Ordering the exact tonnage guarantees a short load, and a partial load arriving an hour later cannot be knitted into the mat properly — it leaves a cold joint that opens up.

4. Size the base in the same order. A residential driveway needs 4 to 6 in of well-compacted crusher run under the asphalt, 6 to 8 in where frost is deep or the soil is soft. For this driveway, 6 in of base is 600 × 0.5 = 300 cu ft = 11.11 cu yd. Crusher run weighs about 1.5 tons per cu yd, so that is 16.67 tons exact, or 19.17 tons with a 15% compaction allowance — order about 20 tons. The base is what carries the load: asphalt is a flexible surface that spreads load over the base, and most driveway failures start in a thin or poorly compacted base. Size it with the Gravel Driveway Calculator.

5. Check the plant minimum. Many suppliers have a 1 to 3 ton minimum load, so a small patch may cost more than its tonnage suggests. A 10 ft × 20 ft pad at 2 in is only 2.66 tons to order — check the minimum before you assume the per-ton price applies.

6. Confirm the density with your supplier. 145 lb per cu ft is the standard estimating figure, but real mixes run 140 to 150 depending on the aggregate and how well it is rolled. If your supplier quotes a different density, use theirs — a 5 lb difference moves the order by about 0.4 tons on this driveway.

7. Order whole tons, and agree the figure with your paver first. Plants sell by the ton and will not split a load finely. Round up, not down, and confirm the tonnage with the crew laying it before the truck is loaded — they know the subgrade and the edges better than any calculator.

Small repairs: cold patch by the bag

For potholes and edges you do not need a truck. Cold patch comes in 50 lb bags, needs no heat, and is tamped in place. A 2 ft × 2 ft pothole at 2 in deep is 4 × (2 ÷ 12) = 0.67 cu ft, times 145 lb per cu ft = about 97 lb — so 2 bags of 50 lb cold patch. Cold patch never reaches the density or lifespan of hot mix, so treat it as a repair, not a driveway, and expect to redo it.

Common mistakes that leave a driveway short

Using loose depth instead of compacted. A quarter of the order, gone — see check 2 above.

Skimping on the base. The base carries the load. A thin base means alligator cracks and ruts within a few years, and adding more asphalt on top of a bad base buys a couple of years at most.

Paving over soft or wet subgrade. Fix the drainage first, or the pavement fails regardless of thickness. Asphalt must shed water — a minimum of about 2% cross-slope. Flat driveways pond, and standing water is what breaks pavement apart.

Ordering short. Asphalt cools. A second, partial load arriving an hour later cannot be knitted into the mat properly, leaving a cold joint that opens up. Round up and order the whole tons.

Forgetting the slope and the edges. Thickened edges and a proper fall for water are part of the job, not extras — and they take material. If the edges are thickened, add for them before you order.

Method and limits

This guide calculates asphalt tonnage as area × depth, with depth converted from inches to feet by dividing by 12, volume converted to weight at 145 lb per cu ft of compacted hot mix, and weight converted to short tons by dividing by 2,000 — the same formula the BuildCalc Asphalt Calculator runs (tons = area × depth × 0.0060417 × (1 + waste %)). A 10% waste allowance is added, the calculator default. Real mixes run 140 to 150 lb per cu ft depending on aggregate and compaction, so confirm the density with your supplier. The base uses 6 in of crusher run at 1.5 tons per cu yd with a 15% compaction allowance, per the gravel driveway guide. The cost example uses a typical US figure of $120 per ton for hot mix — prices vary by region, supplier and haul distance, and the installed $3 to $7 per sq ft range is a typical planning range, not a quote. This guide counts asphalt and base stone only: it does not choose the tack coat, set the drainage fall, check the subgrade or price excavation, grading, edge restraint, labour or equipment. Treat the total as a planning estimate and confirm the final order with your supplier and your paver before you buy. Frequently asked questions

How many tons of asphalt do I need for a 20x30 driveway?

A 20 ft x 30 ft driveway (600 sq ft) at 3 in compacted needs 10.88 tons of asphalt as exact tonnage, or 11.96 tons to order with a 10% waste allowance — order 12 tons. The volume is 600 x (3 divided by 12) = 150 cu ft, times 145 lb per cu ft = 21,750 lb, divided by 2,000 = 10.88 tons, times 1.10 = 11.96 tons. At $120 per ton that is about $1,436 in asphalt, before the base.

How thick should an asphalt driveway be?

2 to 3 in of compacted asphalt over a 4 to 6 in compacted aggregate base for a residential driveway. Go to 3 to 4 in where light trucks park, and 4 to 6 in for heavy truck traffic. An overlay on sound existing pavement can be 1.5 to 2 in. Thickness is always the compacted depth, not the loose depth spread before rolling.

Does asphalt thickness mean compacted or loose?

Compacted, always — that is the finished depth you specify and pay for. Hot mix compacts by roughly 25% under the roller, so 3 in finished needs about 4 in spread loose. If you enter a loose depth into a calculator you over-order; if you measure the loose mat and treat it as the finished thickness, you finish about a quarter short.

How much base do I need under asphalt?

4 to 6 in of well-compacted crusher run for a residential driveway, 6 to 8 in where frost is deep or the soil is soft. For a 20 ft x 30 ft driveway, 6 in of base is 300 cu ft, or 11.11 cu yd — 16.67 tons of crusher run at 1.5 tons per cu yd, or 19.17 tons with a 15% compaction allowance, so order about 20 tons. The base is what carries the load; most driveway failures start in a thin or poorly compacted base, not in the asphalt.

How much does one ton of asphalt cover?

One ton of hot mix asphalt (2,000 lb at 145 lb per cu ft) covers about 83 sq ft at 2 in compacted and about 55 sq ft at 3 in compacted. Use it as a quick cross-check: a 600 sq ft driveway at 3 in needs about 600 divided by 55 = 10.9 tons before waste, which matches the 10.88-ton exact figure from the full calculation.

Can I lay asphalt myself?

Realistically, no, for anything larger than a patch. Hot mix arrives at around 300 F and has to be spread and rolled before it cools below roughly 185 F, which means a paver, a roller and a crew working fast. For small repairs, cold patch in 50 lb bags works: a 2 ft x 2 ft pothole at 2 in deep needs about 97 lb of cold patch, or 2 bags. Cold patch never reaches the density or lifespan of hot mix, so treat it as a repair, not a driveway.
