How Much Gravel Do I Need for a Shed Base? A Worked Project Walkthrough

A gravel pad is the cheapest solid base a shed can sit on — but only if you size the pad, not the shed. The full walkthrough for a 10x12 ft shed: pad size, depth, stone type, the order in tons and cubic yards, the build steps in order, and the two mistakes that leave a shed sitting crooked.

Short answer

A 10 ft × 12 ft shed needs 2.28 cubic yards of gravel to order — a 12 ft × 14 ft pad, 4 inches deep, with 10% extra for compaction and handling. That is 3.2 tons of clean #57 stone or 4.1 tons of crusher run. At the example prices in this guide the whole base costs about $264 all in, stone, fabric, timber edging and delivery included. The formula

Pad length = shed length + 2 ft (12 inches past the shed on every side)

Pad width = shed width + 2 ft

Volume (cu yd) = pad length × pad width × (depth in inches ÷ 12) ÷ 27

To order = volume × 1.10 (10% extra for compaction and handling)

Tons = cubic yards to order × 1.40 for clean #57 stone, or × 1.80 for crusher run

Four steps, in this order. First, make the pad bigger than the shed — 12 inches past every wall — because the stone you order covers the pad, and a pad sized to the shed leaves the walls sitting on the edge. Second, work out the volume in cubic yards: length times width times depth in feet, divided by 27. Third, add 10% — compaction squeezes the stone down, so the loose volume you order is more than the finished depth. Fourth, convert to tons with the weight of your stone type, because most yards sell by the ton. Crusher run is heavier per yard than clean stone, so the same pad costs more tons in crusher run.

The worked example: a 10 ft × 12 ft shed, 4 inches deep

A 10 ft × 12 ft shed on firm, well-drained soil, with 3/4 inch clean #57 stone, 4 inches compacted, 10% extra.

Step 1 — Pad size. Add 1 ft on every side: 10 + 2 = 12 ft, 12 + 2 = 14 ft. The pad is 12 × 14 ft, 168 sq ft — 40% more area than the 120 sq ft shed footprint.

Step 2 — Volume. 4 inches is 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft. 12 × 14 × 0.333 = 56 cu ft. 56 ÷ 27 = 2.07 cu yd net.

Step 3 — Order quantity. Add 10% for compaction and handling: 2.07 × 1.10 = 2.28 cu yd to order.

Step 4 — Tons. Clean #57 stone at 1.40 tons per cubic yard: 2.28 × 1.40 = 3.19 tons, so order about 3.2 tons. Crusher run at 1.80 tons per cubic yard: 2.28 × 1.80 = 4.10 tons, so order about 4.1 tons.

Step 5 — Bags, if you buy bagged. A 50 lb bag is 0.025 tons, so 3.19 tons is 3.19 × 2,000 ÷ 50 = about 128 bags. That is why a shed pad is a bulk-delivery job: 128 bags cost far more than one bulk load and take a day to haul. Order loose stone by the ton.

Step 6 — Price it. At the example price of $50 per cubic yard, 2.28 cu yd of stone costs $114. Add about $30 of heavy-duty landscape fabric and about $45 of ground-contact pressure-treated timber for the frame, and the materials come to about $189. With an example $75 delivery, the base costs about $264 all in. These are example prices, not a quote — get a price from your local supplier before you order.

Cross-check with a bigger shed at the deeper end: a 12 ft × 16 ft shed on soft ground needs a 14 ft × 18 ft pad at 6 inches. 14 × 18 = 252 sq ft. 6 inches is 0.5 ft: 252 × 0.5 = 126 cu ft, 126 ÷ 27 = 4.67 cu yd net, plus 10% = 5.13 cu yd to order. Clean stone: 5.13 × 1.40 = 7.19 tons, about 7.2 tons. Crusher run: 5.13 × 1.80 = 9.24 tons, about 9.2 tons. Stone alone at the example $50 per cubic yard is 5.13 × $50 = about $257. Crusher run vs #57 stone for a shed base

Both are angular crushed stone and both work under a shed — the difference is the fines. Crusher run is stone mixed with stone dust straight off the crusher, so it packs into the firmest pad. #57 is washed stone with the fines screened out, so it drains faster but needs the frame to hold it.

Stone names and size numbers vary by region and by quarry, and the same product can go by two different names an hour apart. Rather than relying on a number, describe what you want at the yard: angular crushed stone with the fines left in for the firm pad, or washed angular stone around 3/4 inch with no fines for the fast-draining one. Building the pad: eight steps in order

1. Mark the pad. String lines and stakes at the pad size — 12 ft × 14 ft for a 10 × 12 ft shed. Check the corners are square by measuring both diagonals; they must match.

2. Dig out 4 to 6 inches of soil. Strip the sod first, then dig to the compacted depth. Before you dig, check local setback rules and whether your town needs a permit for the shed.

3. Compact the soil. Run a plate compactor or hand tamper over the bare soil. Dampen it lightly and compact again until it is firm — soft soil under stone is how pads sink.

4. Lay landscape fabric. Heavy-duty fabric over the compacted soil, overlapping the joints. It keeps weeds down and stops the stone sinking into the soil. Never plastic sheeting — it blocks drainage.

5. Build the timber frame. Ground-contact pressure-treated timbers around the pad edge hold the stone in place and give you a screed edge to level against. Set the frame level.

6. Spread in lifts. Do not dump 6 inches of stone and tamp the top — the bottom stays loose. Spread 2 to 3 inches, wet it lightly, compact it, then do the next layer. Spread about 5 inches loose to finish at 4 inches compacted.

7. Level it. Drag a straight board across the frame to screed the stone flat. A shed floor needs flat more than it needs perfectly level — check with a long level in both directions.

8. Set the skids or blocks. Treated 4x4 skids or concrete blocks sit on the compacted stone and carry the shed floor. Check your shed builder's site-prep sheet first: some builders publish a pad spec, and getting it wrong can cost a redelivery fee or void a floor warranty. Planning the floor itself? Two mistakes that leave a shed sitting crooked

Sizing the shed, not the pad. A 10 × 12 ft shed covers 120 sq ft, but its 12 × 14 ft pad covers 168 sq ft — 40% more. Ordering for the shed footprint buys 2.07 cubic yards short of the pad by a third, and the walls end up sitting on the pad edge where the stone spreads. Always enter the pad size in the calculator.

Using pea gravel. Rounded pea gravel looks tidy in a bag, but round stones roll past each other instead of locking together, so the pad never packs firm and the shed settles unevenly. Angular crushed stone is the base; save the pea gravel for the path to the shed door — size that with the Pea Gravel Calculator. Drainage around the shed matters too: if water collects at the site, read the Drainage Gravel Calculator guide before you dig.

Method and limits

The formula is pad length = shed length + 2 ft and pad width = shed width + 2 ft, then volume = pad length × pad width × (depth in inches ÷ 12) ÷ 27, to order = volume × 1.10, and tons = cubic yards × 1.40 for clean #57 stone or × 1.80 for crusher run — the same approach the Gravel Patio Calculator uses. Depth is a planning range of 4 to 6 inches of compacted stone: 4 inches suits a small shed on firm, well-drained soil, 6 inches suits soft ground, clay, wet sites, or a heavy shed. The stone weights are typical values — your quarry's stone can be lighter or heavier, so confirm the tons-per-yard figure with your supplier before you order, and confirm the final quantity with them too. The $50 per cubic yard stone price, $30 fabric, $45 timber and $75 delivery are example prices, not quotes. Frequently asked questions

How much gravel do I need for a 10x12 shed base?

A 10 ft by 12 ft shed needs a 12 ft by 14 ft pad, 12 inches wider on every side. At 4 inches compacted that is 2.07 cubic yards net, so order 2.28 cubic yards with 10% extra — 3.2 tons of clean #57 stone or 4.1 tons of crusher run. At 6 inches the same pad needs 3.42 cubic yards to order.

How big should a gravel pad be for a shed?

About 12 inches wider than the shed on every side, so the pad is 2 feet longer and 2 feet wider than the shed itself. A 10 ft by 12 ft shed gets a 12 ft by 14 ft pad. The border keeps roof runoff from splashing mud onto the siding, keeps grass away from the walls, and gives you room to square the floor.

How deep should gravel be under a shed?

Plan on 4 to 6 inches of compacted crushed stone. Four inches is enough for a small shed on firm, well-drained soil. Go to 6 inches on soft clay, wet ground, or under a heavy shed. Measure the compacted depth, not the loose depth the truck delivers — spread about 5 inches loose to finish at 4 inches compacted.

What gravel is best for a shed base?

Angular crushed stone, commonly 3/4 inch clean stone such as #57, or crusher run. The sharp edges lock together when compacted and make a firm, level surface. Rounded pea gravel does not pack down and rolls underfoot, so it is a poor choice under a shed — use it for paths and beds instead.

How many tons of gravel for a shed base?

It depends on the pad size, the depth and the stone type. Clean #57 stone weighs about 1.40 tons per cubic yard and crusher run about 1.80 tons. A 10 ft by 12 ft shed at 4 inches needs 3.2 tons of clean stone or 4.1 tons of crusher run; a 12 ft by 16 ft shed at 6 inches needs 7.2 tons of clean stone or 9.2 tons of crusher run.

Do I need landscape fabric under a gravel shed pad?

It helps. A layer of heavy-duty landscape fabric over the compacted soil keeps weeds down and stops the gravel from sinking into the soil over time. Do not use plastic sheeting instead — it blocks drainage and water will pool under the shed.

How much does a gravel shed base cost?

At the example prices in this guide — $50 per cubic yard of stone, $30 of landscape fabric, $45 of timber edging and $75 delivery — a 10 ft by 12 ft shed base costs about $264 all in. The stone alone is about $114. These are example prices, not a quote, so get a price from your local supplier before you order.
