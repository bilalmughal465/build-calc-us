How Much Sod Do I Need for a Lawn? A Checklist With a Worked Example

A 40x25 ft lawn worked from tape measure to rolls to pallets to cost — the area formula, the waste allowance everyone forgets, the sod for every common lawn size, the roll and pallet sizes suppliers actually sell, and the sod-vs-seed decision, with every number shown so you can check it against your own lawn.

Short answer

A 40 ft × 25 ft lawn needs 1,100 sq ft of sod to order — 110 standard rolls of 10 sq ft, or 3 pallets of 450 sq ft — with a 10% waste allowance. The lawn itself is 1,000 sq ft, and every curve and cut turns full rolls into offcuts, so ordering the net area leaves the lawn short. At the example price of $0.50 per sq ft that order costs about $550 before delivery. Work the checklist for your own lawn, or size the order with the Sod Calculator. The checklist: 8 steps before you order

Step 1 — Measure each section. Measure the lawn in feet, one section at a time. A rectangle is length × width. A circle is π × radius². A triangle is ½ × base × height. Most lawns are not one clean shape, so break yours into rectangles, circles and triangles, work each one out, and add the areas together.

Step 2 — Subtract what will not be turfed. Take out the flower beds, paths, patio edges and driveway strips. A 20 ft × 30 ft lawn is 600 sq ft, and taking out a 50 sq ft bed leaves 550 sq ft net — ordering for the full 600 buys sod you will throw away.

Step 3 — Pick your waste allowance. Use 5% for a plain rectangle, 10% where there are curves, beds or paths to cut around, and up to 15% for a heavily curved lawn with many beds, diagonal cuts or sprinkler heads to work past. The allowance covers cuts, curves and the odd damaged piece — it is standard practice, not padding.

Step 4 — Convert to rolls. A standard roll is 2 ft × 5 ft and covers 10 sq ft. Divide the area to order by 10 and round up — you cannot buy part of a roll. 1,100 sq ft to order is 110 rolls.

Step 5 — Convert to pallets. Pallets are sold whole, so divide the area to order by the pallet coverage and round up to the next whole pallet. At the common 450 sq ft per pallet, 1,100 sq ft is 2.44 pallets, so you order 3 pallets. Being just over a pallet step is the most common surprise in a sod order — trimming the area or the waste allowance slightly can sometimes drop a whole pallet.

Step 6 — Confirm the roll and pallet sizes with your supplier. Roll and pallet coverage vary by grower, grass type and how the sod is cut. A pallet is 450 to 500 sq ft at most farms, but some cut smaller slabs — ask for the exact figures on the quote before you order, because the piece count changes with them even though the area does not.

Step 7 — Price it. Multiply the area to order by the price per square foot. At the example price of $0.50 per sq ft, 1,100 sq ft costs $550. Add delivery, which is often charged per pallet or per trip, and installation if you are not laying it yourself.

Step 8 — Plan the laying day. Sod is living grass and it starts to dry out while it sits rolled up. Order for the day you will lay it, have the ground prepared first, and water each section as soon as it is laid — do not leave pallets sitting in the sun while you finish the prep.

The worked example: a 40 ft × 25 ft front lawn

Area. 40 × 25 = 1,000 sq ft net. No beds or paths to subtract on this lawn.

Waste. The lawn has a curved edge along the driveway, so 10%: 1,000 × 1.10 = 1,100 sq ft to order.

Rolls. 1,100 ÷ 10 = 110 standard rolls of 2 ft × 5 ft.

Pallets. 1,100 ÷ 450 = 2.44, so 3 pallets — the order is 1,350 sq ft of pallet capacity for 1,100 sq ft of lawn, and the spare rolls are the ones you reach for when a piece is damaged or a corner needs re-cutting.

Cost. 1,100 × $0.50 = $550 at the example price, before delivery.

Weight. Freshly cut sod weighs about 5 lb per sq ft, so 1,100 sq ft weighs about 5,500 lb, or 2.75 tons — a full pallet is about 2,250 lb, over a ton, so this is a delivery-truck job, not a car load. Wet sod weighs noticeably more.

Cross-check with a second lawn: a 20 ft × 30 ft lawn is 600 sq ft, less a 50 sq ft bed is 550 sq ft net, and at 5% waste that is 577.5 sq ft to order — 58 rolls and 2 pallets — the same figures the sod calculators other sites publish for those inputs, so the arithmetic holds. Sod vs seed: which one fits your lawn

Sod is grass grown on a farm and cut with a thin layer of soil and roots, so it lays straight onto prepared ground and gives you a finished lawn the day you lay it. It costs more — at the example price of $0.50 per sq ft, a 1,000 sq ft lawn is about $500 in material — and it is heavy to move and must be laid the day it arrives. Size the seed side of the decision with the Lawn Seed Calculator.

Seed costs a fraction of sod per square foot, so it is far cheaper for a large lawn, and it is light to carry and easy to store. The trade-off is time: seed needs weeks of watering and care before it fills in into a usable lawn, and a heavy rain can wash it off a slope before it takes. If the budget is tight and you can wait, seed wins. If you need a lawn now, sod wins.

Either way, the ground underneath decides the result. Sod laid on unprepared ground dries out and never roots properly. Level the area, remove stones and old grass, and if the ground needs building up, size the fill with the topsoil guide and the Topsoil Calculator — and plan the watering with the Sprinkler Calculator and the feeding with the Lawn Fertilizer Calculator.

What changes the answer for sod

Your shape. A rectangle is length × width, but a curved lawn needs the waste allowance raised to 10% or 15%, and an L-shaped lawn should be split into two rectangles and added together.

Your exclusions. Beds, paths and patios come off the net area before the waste allowance is added. Forgetting a 50 sq ft bed on a 600 sq ft lawn over-orders by about 5 rolls.

Your waste allowance. 5% for a plain rectangle, 10% for curves and beds, 15% for heavy cutting. Skipping it entirely is the single most common reason a lawn comes up short.

Your roll and pallet sizes. The area to order does not change, but the roll count and the pallet count do. A farm cutting 2.67 sq ft slabs instead of 10 sq ft rolls turns 110 rolls into 413 slabs for the same lawn — same area, very different order.

Your grass type. Sod weight and price move with the grass type, the thickness of the cut and how wet it is. A pallet of thick-cut wet sod weighs noticeably more than a pallet of thin-cut dry sod, so check the weight before you plan the haul.

Common mistakes that leave a lawn short

Ordering the net area with no waste allowance. Every curve and every corner turns a full roll into an offcut that usually cannot be reused. 5% to 15% extra is standard practice, not padding.

Measuring the whole plot instead of the lawn. The beds, paths and patio are not lawn. Measure the grass area only, or subtract the exclusions before you add waste.

Assuming a pallet is always 500 sq ft. Pallet coverage is 450 to 500 sq ft at most farms and varies by grower. Assuming 500 when your supplier cuts 450 leaves you a tenth of a pallet short.

Rounding down. Sod is sold whole. 2.44 pallets is an order for 3 pallets, not 2 — and the spare rolls are the ones you reach for when a piece is damaged.

Leaving sod rolled up in the sun. Cut sod heats up and dries out fast while it sits rolled. Lay it the same day it arrives and water each section as you go.

Method and limits

This guide calculates sod as area = length × width for a rectangle, π × radius² for a circle and ½ × base × height for a triangle, then area to order = net area × (1 + waste ÷ 100), rolls = area to order ÷ 10 sq ft per standard roll, rounded up, and pallets = area to order ÷ pallet coverage, rounded up — the same formula the BuildCalc Sod Calculator runs. The standard roll is 2 ft × 5 ft (10 sq ft) and the common pallet figure is 450 sq ft, but both vary by supplier, so confirm them on your quote. The waste allowance is 5% for a plain rectangle, 10% for curves and beds and up to 15% for heavy cutting. Sod weight uses about 5 lb per square foot, so a 450 sq ft pallet is about 2,250 lb — wet sod weighs more, so check with your supplier before you plan the haul. The $0.50 per sq ft figure is the guide's example price, not a quoted market price, and sod prices vary a lot by grass type, region and supplier. This guide counts sod area only: it does not price delivery, installation, ground preparation, topsoil, seed, fertilizer, watering or labour, and it does not cover your soil quality, drainage or which grass type suits your climate. Frequently asked questions

How much sod do I need for 1,000 sq ft?

A 1,000 sq ft lawn needs 1,000 sq ft of sod net. Add 5% for cutting on a plain rectangle and you order 1,050 sq ft — 105 standard rolls of 10 sq ft, or 3 pallets of 450 sq ft. With 10% waste for curves and beds, order 1,100 sq ft, which is 110 rolls and still 3 pallets.

How many rolls of sod are on a pallet?

A pallet usually holds 45 to 50 standard rolls, covering 450 to 500 sq ft. The figure varies by grower, grass type and how the sod is cut, so confirm the pallet coverage on your quote before you order — a pallet is sold whole, so rounding up to the next pallet is part of the order.

How much does sod cost per square foot?

Sod commonly costs around $0.30 to $0.80 per sq ft for the material alone, depending on grass type and region, so a 1,000 sq ft lawn is roughly $300 to $800 before delivery and installation. This guide uses $0.50 per sq ft as an example price: 1,100 sq ft to order costs about $550. Get a quote from your local grower, because prices vary a lot by area.

How much extra sod should I order?

Order about 5% extra for a simple rectangular lawn, 10% where there are curves, beds or paths to cut around, and up to 15% for a heavily curved lawn with many beds, diagonal cuts or sprinkler heads to work past. Every curve turns a full roll into an offcut that usually cannot be reused, so skipping the allowance leaves the lawn short.

Is sod or grass seed cheaper?

Seed costs a fraction of sod per square foot, so it is far cheaper for a large lawn. Sod costs more but gives you a finished lawn the day you lay it, while seed needs weeks of watering and care before it fills in. If the budget is tight and you can wait, seed wins; if you need a lawn now, sod wins.

How soon do I have to lay sod after delivery?

Lay it the same day it arrives. Sod is living grass cut from the ground, and it starts to dry out and heat up while it sits rolled up — do not leave pallets sitting in the sun. Water each section as soon as it is laid, and water the whole lawn deeply on the first day.
